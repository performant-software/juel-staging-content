Bernard Gaines Farrar Jr., the son of Dr. Bernard Gaines and Ann Clark Thruston, was born in St. Louis, Missouri, on August 5, 1831. He was born into a Missouri slaveholding family. He studied first at Norwich Military Academy in Vermont, returned to Missouri to attend Saint Louis University, and then studied at the University of Virginia from 1850 to 1852. 

Upon completing his studies at UVA, Farrar moved to Philadelphia. He became active in the Free Soil Party in 1854. Still living in Pennsylvania, he married Isabel Jerdone Mitchell in 1855. By 1860, Farrar and his family had returned to St. Louis. At the war's outset in 1861, he became quite active in organizing the city in support of union. He would enlist in the United States Army in October 1862. Border state Missouri, riven by conflict between pro-slavery (and thus pro-Confederate) elements and strong pro-union sentiment, remained committed neither to the United States nor to the Confederacy. Farrar's home of St. Louis, however, remained a hotbed of pro-Union sentiment in the state, possibly in part due to the city's large German immigrant population.

By May 1862, Farrar was a Major in the U.S. Army. He would soon be promoted and appointed Aide de Camp to General Nathaniel Lyon. In his first couple months working under Lyon, Farrar participated in a campaign to drive out Missouri’s pro-Confederate government. The regiment’s first major advance was in thwarting a plan to seize a U.S. arsenal in the state. Lyon’s forces surrounded Camp Jackson and Major Farrar delivered the demand for surrender to Confederate officer General Frost. The Confederate forces surrendered without a fight.

Later that year, he took command of a brigade at the siege of Vicksburg. Farrar was once again quickly promoted, becoming Missouri’s provost-marshal-general. In that role, he sought to limit dissent, including disbanding the Chamber of Commerce, as part of his effort to prevent the southern-sympathizing Missourians from meeting and or conspiring to undermine U.S. military efforts to preserve the American union. 

Farrar, after the war claimed that while he was fighting in Mississippi in 1862 and 1863, he led local plantations, freeing enslaved men who agreed to join the U.S. Army. Those men, along with others who ran to union lines, were organized into a military unit in Corinth, Mississippi. By 1864, Farrar was promoted to colonel and at Grant's direction formed the 6th U.S. Colored Heavy Artillery, an all Black unit that likely included men he had helped to liberate from Mississippi plantations.

At the war's end in 1865, Farrar retired as a brevet brigadier general. He quickly became active in post-war Republican politics in Missouri. In 1891, President Benjamin Harrison appointed him Assistant U.S. Treasurer for Missouri, a position he held for four years. He also remained active in U.S. veterans organizations for the rest of his life. He would die in Saint Louis in 1916 just two months after his wife Isabel died. They are both buried in a family tomb at Bellefontaine Cemetery in Saint Louis.
