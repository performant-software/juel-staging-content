John B. Minor ([P22874](/en/people/f624eefa-050a-46a9-a735-298a6fe3ce23)) was a prominent legal professor at the University of Virginia for fifty years.  In addition to this position, Minor also enslaved many people right up until the general emancipation in 1865.  With the decline in the profitability plantation agriculture (tobacco, corn, and wheat) in many parts of Virginia during the mid-nineteenth century, the practice of renting enslaved became more and more common.  

In late 1862, less than a year into the Civil War, Minor rented enslaved man Phil ([P48456](/en/people/10a7e77e-db95-4a4e-ab14-b4c25b71e796)) to Randolph Turk ([P48313](/en/people/8de50631-bfd6-4747-a38b-6a63e45608b3)), the Confederate Superintendent and Administrator of the Quarter Masters Office in Staunton. After six months of labor and life forty miles from his family, Phil chose to flee Staunton, most likely to return to family and community, but possibly in a desperate attempt at self-emancipation by running to the United States military. 

Turk,  in a March 4th, 1863, letter, opined he knew “of no reason for his leaving," but Virginia enslavers often deluded themselves into thinking the people they enslaved were “happy” with the arrangement. They also evinced little interest in the interior lives of the people they held in bondage, and Turk here was no exception. He likely had not thought a moment about Phil. 

UVA student Catherine S. Neale, in her 2006 thesis, theorized that Phil ran away as a form of protest against the Confederacy as he was working in direct aid of the war effort.  But Turk himself had the more likely explanation: Phil was running back to Charlottesville rather than towards freedom in Virginia territory held by the U.S. Army. As the story unfolded, it becomes clear that Phil, while surely harboring dreams of emancipation, was responding to more immediate concerns—fleeing harsh conditions in Staunton and returning to family and community in Charlottesville. 

Phil, as part of his escape, stowed himself away on a train heading to Charlottesville. Unfortunately for Phil, the train car he had hidden in was detached at Mechums River Western Albemarle County, so it never made it to Charlottesville. Phil was soon captured and returned to Minor in Charlottesville. Phil did not successfully self-emancipate, and did not make it back to his family and community in quite the way he surely imagined when he fled Staunton. But he did end up back with Minor in Charlottesville. Minor chose not to return Phil once more to Turk in Staunton. Instead, Minor sent another enslaved man, Henry, to Turk, as a replacement for Phil.
