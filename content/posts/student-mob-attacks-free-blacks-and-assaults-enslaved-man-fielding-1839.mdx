On Monday, 25th February 1839, Chairman of the Faculty Gessner Harrison received a visit from a distraught Mathematics Professor Charles Bonnycastle.  Bonnycastle reported that the day before he had been urgently notified that Fielding, an enslaved man who worked as a domestic in Bonnycastle's Pavilion, was being “murdered” by some students just north of university along the road to Charlottesville.  

Bonnycastle, seeking to protect his property, ran to the scene of the mob beating. There, he discovered Fielding being held down by a student named Madison McAfee. When Bonnycastle arrived, McAfee had raised a club and was poised continue savagely beating Fielding’s bruised body.  Bonnycastle, attempting to intervene by grabbing McAfee. McAfee, further enraged by the professor's interference,  responded with threats, insults, and renewed clubbing of Fielding. Other students, supporting McAfee, pulled Bonnycastle away. Another student, Franklin English, physically restrained Bonnycastle so McAfee could continue with the beating.  Eventually, Mr. Bonnycastle’s protestations succeeded as he broke free and momentarily stopped McAfee’s assault. In that instant, Fielding wriggled free, but not before English delivered a few brutal blows to him.

Bonnycastle took his complaint to the faculty chairman, both because he understood his role as a professor and master to all students, but also because the savage beating had injured Fielding and thus damaged Bonnycastle's human property. 

Chairman Gessner Harrison next summoned McAfee and English to his office. English testified that he had been on his way to Charlottesville when he had encountered a group of free Black men, two of whom were fighting each other while the rest gathered around to watch and cheer them on.  Having taken it upon himself as a white master of men to break up the fracas, English intervened and was joined by a mob of students who began attacking the free Black men. English claimed he had been subjected to verbal abuse by Fieldling, who objected to being the student attack on the free Black men. English also testified that he then struck Fielding with a club repeatedly, but could subdue Fielding, so he yelled to his friend McAfee to help him.  The pair of students then set about punishing Fielding for his supposed insolence, holind him down and savagely beaitng him with clubs. Bonnycastle intervened a few minutes later.

McAfee testified that during the beating of Fielding, he felt someone roughly seize his collar, not yet knowing it was professor Bonnycastle. McAfee argued that Bonnycastle was lucky that had not responded by punching the person who had grabbed his collar.  McAfee also stated that he had told Bonnycastle that if he “interfered to protect his servant in his insolence, he was the greater rascal of the two.”  English, explaining why he had come to lay hands upon and physically restrain a professor, asserted that he had only held Bonnycastle back in order to protect him from McAfee’s righteous anger.  Both students admitted that they had jostled Bonnycastle and used language that was disrespectful to the professor, but they argued that, through his own behavior, Bonnycastle had “thrown off the garb of a Professor and assumed that of a man recklessly interposing to screen his servant from merited chastisement.”

Over the next few days, Chairman Harrison went about collecting statements from several witnesses to the incident, hoping to gain some clarity as to where blame should be apportioned in the affair.  Harrison then reported back to the full faculty on March 2.  Several witnesses testified that Fielding had indeed been insolent and disrespectful to the students, which in turn had provoked them to punish him with a beating. Witnesses also indicated that  McAfee and English had abused the Professor only after he had intervened, and that McAfee had directed inappropriate language towards Bonnycastle. One witness did indicate that Fielding's “insolence” amounted to attempting to intervene as English, McAfee and other students attacked the free Black men. According to those witnesses, Fielding had simply pleaded that the men were not enslaved, they were free, they were not on university property, and the students should leave them alone. 

 After due consideration of the facts of the case, the Faculty came to a unanimous decision. The students, McAfee and English, would not be subjected to disciplinary action, the Faculty being of the opinion that the “peculiar circumstances” of the incident excused their disrespect to Mr. Bonnycastle, while the vicious beating that they had dealt to the slave Fielding, it seems, would be considered justified. As both faculty and students were participants in Virginia slave society's culture of mastery, they interpreted any form of imagined resistance by an enslaved person as “insolence.” The faculty further revealed their views on violence against enslaved people when they indicated they would report the incident to the local court to determine if Fielding merited legal repercussions. 

The single account that Fielding was intervening to protect men who were not enslaved does provide evidence about how enslaved communities at UVA were keenly aware of their status as human property, understood that not all Black Virginians were enslaved, and that free people of any color had rights. Enslaved people at UVA, including Fielding and many others, dreamt of freedom and often had rather astute understandings of the terrain of political economy in Virginia and beyond.
