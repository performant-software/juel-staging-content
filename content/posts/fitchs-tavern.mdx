William D. Fitch ran a tavern in the Dyer House on the corner of Seventh and East Jefferson Streets in Charlottesville. Fitch's tavern and others became flashpoints for UVA faculty as they attempted to rein in studying behavior. The tavern hosted dances for the general public on several occasions, though students had to obtain permission from the faculty to attend. In fact, only five out of twenty five tickets were available to UVA students for a dance thrown there in December 1838. The tickets cost $2-$3 and students, according to UVA enactments, were required to be in uniform. These dances also appear to have been a chance for the male students to interact with women in the area. For example, Reuben Thomas, a second year, brought a date to a dance at Fitch’s in February 1840.

Students, rebelling against strict faculty rules governing their behavior, often did not obtain permission from the faculty to attend dances or visit town. UVA records are filled with reference to disciplinary violations for drinking at Fitch's or other taverns and for being found out of uniform in public. Such was the case for Joseph B. Cosnahan, who was suspended for two weeks in 1839 and required to spend that time at Ira B. Brown’s in Charlottesville (a tavern and hotel). 

It was not uncommon for students to get alcohol at Fitch’s tavern and return to Grounds intoxicated. In 1829,  many students drunkenly returned from a dance at Fitch’s tavern late one evening. Professor Gessner Harrison, disturbed at his Pavilion by their noisy return, reported them to the faculty chairman. This incident reveals that student behavior was often far more menacing than simply visiting taverns, refusing to wear the school uniform, or drinking. Professor Harrison reported hearing the students making “indecent propositions made to a female servant” at his Pavilion that night. Those students, already exercising their mastery by ignoring university rules as they partied out of uniforn in town, returned to UVA intoxicated and immediately set about attempting to sexually assault an enslaved woman living in one of the basement rooms adjacent to Harrison's residence. The students were all reprimanded, but the threat of sexual violence against enslaved women remained endemic in the Academical Village. 

Fitch’s tavern, like Ira Brown's and other taverns, was also a hotel. Students, when temporarily suspended from the university, often boarded at the very taverns where they broke university rules. For example, during the 1846-1847 academic year, Henry L. Stevens and William F. Ravenel were granted permission to board at Fitch's with their cousin, Julius DuBose, who was also a student at the University. That same year, the faculty also granted Horace Shifflet’s guardian, Benjamin Sneed, permission to have Shifflet board at Fitch’s.
