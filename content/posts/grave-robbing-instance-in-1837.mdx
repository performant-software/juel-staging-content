Robert E. Nelson of Louisa County, Virginia was a first year student of Chemistry, Medicine, and Anatomy during the twelfth session from 1836-1837. Friends of Nelson’s, Thomas Baker and William Hackett, came to him on Monday, January 9, 1837 to inform him “old Yeargean, the old man who has abstracted himself from society so long, was dead.” Baker and Hackett’s motive for alerting Nelson to Mr. Yeargean’s death did not come from grief or sorrow, but scientific curiosity.  The two boys came to convince Nelson to exhume Mr. Yeargean’s body so that the trio might study it in preparation for their upcoming exam.

Grave robbing, or bodysnatching, was a common way to illicitly acquire cadavers for anatomical study and dissection before the twentieth century. Most often, the victims of grave robbing were deceased African Americans, who had no legal protections in slave society. Less common were instances of bodysnatching that targeted recently deceased white people. Even less common was writing down and noting the name of victim, as Nelson does in this incident.

John “Johnny” Yeargain (spelling variants included Yeargan, Yeargean, Yeargain, Yeargin, and Yurgan), was a white man of German descent, possibly from Pennsylvania or the Tidewater Region of Virginia. Yeargean came to Charlottesville in 1796 as a “young dandy." However, as one newspaper claims, his aspirations for a full social life were soon dashed after an unfortunate prank was played on him at a town dance. After that, Yeargain shut himself in his home at 220 Court Square and was known for selling high quality whiskey and being notoriously frugal. His home was rumored to have bars on the windows and a chain on the door so that the door could be opened just wide enough to pass a bottle of whiskey through. Yeargain hoarded the money he earned from his business and supposedly buried his riches in the basement of his home for safekeeping. Old and discolored coins became known as “Johnny Yeargains” around Charlottesville.

*Yeargain's Home at 220 Court Square in Charlottesville. Photo taken in 1901.*

Johnny Yeargain died a suspicious death in 1837. His body was found in his home, along with a curious scene that included a trail of coins through his backyard. A rumor circulated that thieves broke in and killed Yeargain, making off with some of his money. Given how well-circulated the rumors of Yeargain’s fortune were, the executor of Yeargain’s estate, William Lee of New Kent, oversaw an excavation of Yeargain's basement to find any buried money. Legend had it that the search turned up $13,000, several gallons of whiskey, and tally stick with 14 notches, leading to speculation that $1,000 was still buried somewhere on the property.

Despite John Yeargain's long tenure in Charlottesville, his property-owning, and the local myth and knowledge about him, dying without family meant that there would be no one to protect his burial plot. UVA medical students at the time had an insatiable appetite for cadavers, even Yeargain's would do.
