Robert Garlick Hill Kean, an 1852 University of Virginia graduate, was already a pro-slavery ideologue when he rose in the Confederate government, becoming Chief of the Confederate Bureau of War. In his wartime diary, Kean's views on slavery and on Black Americans come through loud and clear. He deplored the idea of “negro enlistment” in the Confederate Army. He abhorred any conversation about abolishing slavery, and understood all too clearly that Confederacy continue the fight to separate from the United States because “when independence is lost, slavery is at the same blow destroyed.” 

Kean referred to emancipation as “the \[dislocation] of the foundations of society from which no practical results will be reaped by us.” In 1864, as the war raged on, he worried that “a state of society, in which the negroes had been suddenly freed and given political franchises as citizens, the whites overawed by garrisons ruled by Yankee policy, and humbled by defeat and practical subjugation," would be a disaster he would not countenance. He opined that a country with free Black citienship "was one in which I am unwilling my children shall grow up.” 

After the Civil War's end, Kean continued to comment extensively on the political and social environment of his time. He dedicated a significant portion of his diary comments to examining the ways in which the U.S. Army, sent into southern states enforce and ensure reconstruction, often failed to deliver on promises to freedpeople. As the South faced political, economic, and social upheaval, the United States government offered little framework for either white or black Southerners to navigate the new realities of post-emancipation America. But even so, his anger at Black freedom, his rage at the loss of white political control, and his inability to understand this new world in anything but pre-Civil War ideas about slavery and labor, stand out. Kean complained that ecently freed Black people flocked to Southern towns “garrisoned” by “Yankees,” leading to a decline in the availability of a labor force he and other former Confederates could compel to work for them.

Kean also asserted that instead of working to regulate the hiring practices of plantation owners, or of seeking ways to secure landownership for black citizens, the U.S. Army instead “gave stringent orders that Negroes should remain on the plantations and work.” He apparently missed the fact that the United States government was essentially demanding of freedpeople part of what he wanted (other than acquiescing for former Confederates' desire to return to enslaving poeple). 

Despite his racism, his diary does provide clear detail about how the U.S. government's reconstruction policies unfolded on the ground, and thus help modern researchers understand how both reconstruction could remain an unfinished and incomplete revolution and how quickly the North and the South could craft shared ideas about national reconciliation. In Kean's view, almost immediately after Lee's army surrendered,  U.S. officials began the practice of convict leasing. In towns such as Richmond, where Kean lived, Northern officials “arrested, put in barracks, and hired out \[black citizens] … without consulting their wishes as to employer or price.”

In another chilling account, Kean notes an exchange between a leader of freedpeople in Gordonsville, Virginia, and a Union soldier: “\[the spokesman] enquired ‘what sort of freedom this was? and whether they were not to have land given to them?’ The officer turned to the overseer and asked where the graveyard was. It was pointed out – turning to the negro he said, ‘The only land you will get, or any of you, will be 6x3 feet in that lot, and if you do not behave yourselves properly you will get your share very quickly.’” After noting the exchange in Gordonsville, Kean observed that “Manumission after this fashion will be regarded hereafter, when it has borne its fruits, and the passions of the hour have passed away, as the greatest social crime ever committed on the earth." 

Despite witnessing U.S. policy in action that effectively recreated many of the pre-war realities for Black Virginians, all Kean could do was vent his rage and disappointment about the reality of Black freedom. Kean, along with the that Gordonsville freedman, found themselves asking, albeit for very different reasons, “what sort of freedom” it was that the United States was offering to its new Black citizens.
