From the earliest years of its existence, the University community was intertwined with neighboring communities, its students in particular interacting with neighborhood businesses and individuals, if not always in peaceful ways. Charlottesville's Venable Lane neighborhood and the area later sometimes referred to as "Canada" were often the site of such "town and gown" interactions. The incident detailed below could represent a typical instance of relatively harmless "student mischief" that not only captures the tensions between the disciplining University, its disciplined students, and the citizens of Charlottesville, but also captures important details of this "mixed" environment in which a free Black woman and white neighbors coexisted. The incident also highlights the difficulties of being free, Black, and a woman near the University. Those difficulties were far more than “harmless,” they in fact could involve physical violence, sexual assault, and significant property damage.

The Faculty Minutes recorded Mr. Vandergriff's ([P44032](/en/people/3996e9f4-0ed2-4947-bacc-89fd637122a3 "P44032")) account of student misconduct that occurred on the evening of May 22nd, 1834. He relayed that a band of four or five students travelled down to the neighborhood in question. They stopped first at the house of free Black woman Catherine [Kitty Foster](/en/people/b0a8ceff-8d25-4da2-b6f0-676782c68d02 "Foster, Kitty") ([P44031](/en/people/b0a8ceff-8d25-4da2-b6f0-676782c68d02 "P44031")), where they beat on her door and overturned several flower pots, before moving on to the Vandergriff househould. They threw stones at the dog tied in the yard until Mr. Vandergriff himself came out to scold them, then instead threw their stones at him, forcing him to take cover behind a tree in his yard. When Mrs. Vandergriff came out to stop the trouble, she had to hide behind the house to avoid being struck. Of the students involved, Mr. Vandergriff was able to identify only one, Mr. Thomas L. Patterson ([P32066](/en/people/9ad7a79c-2b49-45b8-b905-0fb568992085 "P32066")), who he later claimed he only threw several stones at the Vandergriff's dogs. 

Patterson did indeed admit to "having been one of a party of students who on the night of the 22nd. inst. made an attack on the house of Mr. Vandergriff, by throwing stones at it & breaking his window," although, interestingly, no mention is made of the mischief done at Catherine Foster's house. On May 31st the Faculty decided the attack on the white Vandergriffs merited discipline and ruled to suspend Patterson for two weeks.

This account reveals minor yet interesting details of this mixed neighborhood, whereby a white couple, the Vandergriffs, lived alongside Catherine  Foster, a free Black woman. Both Foster and Vandergriff were well known to the Faculty, thanks to their interactions with students. 

The Chairman's Journals for Session 13 (Vol. 6, September 3, 1836 - February 17, 1837), for example, reveals an amazing story about one free Black woman's entrepreneurial spirit. At that time, students were caught “firing pistols across the road south of the University, & just out of the precincts, which pistols they allege that they keep out of the precincts. . . But these students on being sent, say that they violate no law - that they neither introduce, keep, nor use pistols within the precincts.” Raised to mastery, UVA students unsurprisingly brought guns to Charlottesville and regularly carried them or used them. What's fascinating here is how a complaint about student gun use just south of the University reveals a bit about the Venable Lane neighborhood. The faculty, in addressing the complaint, noted that the students did not keep their guns at UVA (which was against UVA policy). Instead, they paid someone who lived nearby to house their weapons:  "The place of deposits is, I understand, the house of Kitty Foster ([P44031](/en/people/b0a8ceff-8d25-4da2-b6f0-676782c68d02 "P44031")). Under the law, as it stands, the students may have a magazine of pistols & fire arms across the road, & use them out of the precincts as much as they please." 

What was likely the largest firearms cache in Albemarle County at that time was actually the homestead of a free Black woman. Archaeological excavations at the Foster homestead (now a memorial site) confirmed the story—guns, gun parts, and ammunition were found at the site.

Catherine Foster's entrepreneurial spirit in a slave society, her ability to find ways to earn money and provide for her family, were impressive. She worked as a clothes washer and seamstress, charged students to store their guns, and through those financial arrangements with the university community, created intergenerational wealth for herself, her children, and her grandchildren (her 2 ⅛ acre household would remain in the family until descendants sold it in 1906).
