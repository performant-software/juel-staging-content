Thomas Jefferson was always passionate about education. In 1779, he proposed the first state system of public education with his bill entitled, “A Bill for the More General Diffusion of Knowledge.” Jefferson planned a public school system reflective of truly democratic ideals, in which leadership and opportunity were given to whole communities through education. [\[1\]](#_edn1) Jefferson advocated for a system that would benefit intellectually curious people of all classes, not just the aristocracy who could afford an education. Although it was decades later that Jefferson finally founded the University of Virginia, the ideals he proposed in 1779 were undeniably present in the University’s first system of scholarship for State Students.

As the University of Virginia was coming to fruition, the state of Virginia was contemplating creation of a public education system. Between the establishment of Central College in 1817 and its chartering as the University of Virginia in 1819, the Virginia General Assembly passed a bill in 1818 “for establishing a public system of education.”[\[2\]](#_edn2) The Assembly’s plan extended beyond primary education to the university level, including an outline for a scholarship similar to the one Jefferson proposed in his 1779 bill. 

The extensive selection process for the proposes scholarship program began with gatherings at each county courthouse in the state of all young men whose families could not afford a higher education. The visitors of the local ward school would evaluate the merit, promise, and intelligence of each candidate, finally selecting two from each of the 32 senatorial districts for a five-year collegiate education at the state’s expense. Once the two students completed the preliminary five-year education, they were evaluated again. According to the plan, only the brightest, most impressive of the two students was granted a two-year, all expenses paid education at the university. [\[3\]](#_edn3) The General Assembly effectively established the first merit-based scholarship in Virginia, and Jefferson’s University would be one of the first institutions to participate.

With the terms of the scholarship laid out in Virginia legislation, the next task was instituting the scholarship. Securing funding quickly became a major obstacle. The state was either unable or unwilling to allocate funding for the scholarship. Any suggestion of an education tax was met with skepticism and resistance.[\[4\]](#_edn4) Additionally, the young University simply did not have the finances to educate 32 students free of all charges, especially considering that the first class of matriculates only had 123 students, 108 of which were from Virginia.[\[5\]](#_edn5) So, the scholarship did not materialize. The idea was effectively put on hold for over two decades. The General Assembly would return to creating a state scholarship for students in 1845.

At that time, the Board of Visitors at the University did not believe the state had allocated sufficient funds for educating so many students at UVA. After a visit by members of the General Assembly to the University, the Board addressed these concerns during their January 20, 1846 meeting. [\[6\]](#_edn6) According to the Board, the cost of educating 32 boys in 1846 would be $3,136, “thus educating free of charge ¼ of the whole number of Students” then enrolled.[\[7\]](#_edn7)  This would have been too great a financial burden for the University to bear without increasing the Literary Fund from the state. That Literary Fund had long been a key source of state support for UVA. On February 3, 1845, the Senate of Virginia granted the Literary Fund for the University and additional five thousand dollars plus another ten thousand additional dollars annually, a portion of which was explicitly for “the education of the indigent children of this Commonwealth.”[\[8\]](#_edn8) Two full decades after UVA had opened to its first class of students, a practicable plan for financial aid emerged.

With budget issues resolved, the BoV turned to the practical matters of implementing the scholarship took. The Board expanded upon the regulations provided by the state. One student from each district was allowed to study without paying professor fees, University dues, fines, or penalties. Special care was taken by the University to ensure “that the applicant\[,] his parent\[,] or guardian \[was] not in a situation to incur the expenses of his education.”[\[9\]](#_edn9) 

Two students were housed per dormitory room and each State Student would pay no more than $60 per session for board. UVA accepted State students for a two-year term, subject to the Enactments and all other rules and expectations that paying students were bound by. Vacancies for a given district’s position were advertised in the newspapers of the respective counties. Above all, strong academic and moral character was required of the recipients of the scholarship.

The university decided that land adjoining the Proctor’s house, an area known as Monroe Hill, would be designated for housing the scholarship students. Thus, the State Students, the first recipients of financial aid a the university, would be housed in a segregated facility a short distance from the Academical Village. Their housing location would mark them as students who could not afford to attend the school. 

The first class of State Students arrived at the University in 1846 and included 28 matriculants.[\[10\]](#_edn10) Although extensive guidelines for the selection of the students had been outlined, many logistic challenges quickly came to light. Distributing the State Students among the different academic schools was one such challenge. The Faculty was instructed to “regard the preferences \[students] may indicate,” but the State Students did not have full autonomy over their courses in the way full-paying students did.[\[11\]](#_edn11) 

Eventually UVA established a committee led by professors Gessner Harrison, William McGuffey, Edward Courtenay. Their sole purpose was distributing the State Students among the schools, effectively limiting those students' ability to shape their own education.[\[12\]](#_edn12) State Students were permitted to enroll in up to three schools, preferably the academic schools.[\[13\]](#_edn13) 

Additionally, State Students had a second commitment baked into the scholarship. Those who received a degree from the University pledged to teach in any public or private Virginia school for two years.[\[14\]](#_edn14) This was likely an effort to make public education in Virginia self-sustaining. Another challenge was keeping the spots for each district filled and informing districts of vacancies. The Chairman was charged with preparing “an advertisement embodying full information” of the scholarship and distributing it to various Virginia newspapers.[\[15\]](#_edn15) Periodically State Students would decline the appointment, withdraw, or were asked not to return to the University, leaving vacancies in their district. In these cases an advertisement would be posted in local newspapers of the district that contained the vacancy.

Occasionally, information about a State Student’s financial situation would surface that put his scholarship in jeopardy. These instances reveal quite a bit about politics and power in a slave society. One such case in 1854 involved James Wyatt Mills of Greene County. A fellow student informed Professor Harrison that Mr. Mills’ father “owned land and negroes sufficient to enable him to afford the expenses of his son at the university, if he chose.” The Faculty received a letter with evidence of Mr. Mills’ wealth from Mr. F. D. Cunningham. Mr. Mills allegedly enslaved eighteen people: “two valuable women, two almost worthless, and the remaining six under 12 years of age some of them infants,” The remaining eight were inherited from the mother of Mrs. Mills, two of which were “valuable hands and the remainder, young negroes under 12 years of age." The total value of Mr. Mills’ estate was estimated to be over $3,000.[\[16\]](#_edn16) After consideration of the circumstances, the Chairman of the Faculty decided that, despite his apparent wealth, Mr. Mills’ large family prevented him from affording an education at the University. James Mills was allowed to continue attending the University as a State Student.

In the decade after the scholarship was established, enrollment for both the University and the State’s public education system increased. With more students wanting an education, the State passed a law in March 1856 to increase the number of State Students at the University of Virginia from thirty-two to fifty. The general rules and guidelines of the scholarship remained unchanged. One State Student was still selected from each senatorial district, and a student was selected from the state at large if no recipient was chosen from a specific district.[\[17\]](#_edn17) 

The University had no objection to this increase; however, dormitory space became an issue. The State Students were originally housed separately from other students, in dormitories near the Proctor’s house.[\[18\]](#_edn18) These arrangements would not be sufficient to house twenty-eight additional students. The University needed new dormitories but did not have the budget. The Board of Visitors, seeking to generate the finances to build additional segregated dormitory space for the State Students, turned to their own assets as a way to generate quick money.

Albemarle County planter Martin Dawson had left his plantation land to the University in 1835. The farm, known as Dawson’s Farm in UVA records (Bellair Plantation), had been used by UVA as a source of timber and firewood for a decade. The land was given to the University for use strictly as a source of wood, so legally the University could not use that land for any other purpose. When the scholarship started in 1846, Dawson’s Farm was leased along with the Proctor’s House to an individual who would use it for housing State Students. The Board of Visitors hoped that giving the tenant permission to harvest wood on Dawson’s Farm would keep the cost of housing the State Students below $60, while still complying with the legally mandated use. This plan likely never worked well—the plantation was miles from UVA, making it a poor location for student boarding. As well, over the years since UVA had taken possession of the plantation, Dawson's farm had declined due to neglect.

In 1856 the Board saw an opportunity to secure much-needed funds to support the expanded State Student population by selling the land.  The Virginia General Assembly granted the University permission to sell Dawson’s Farm on March 15, 1858.[\[19\]](#_edn19) One year later Albemarle planter and merchant Slaughter W. Ficklin bought the land for just over $19,000.[\[20\]](#_edn20) The profits from that sale were used to construct six new dorms on the southwestern side of Grounds. These dormitories, known as Dawson’s Row, provided forty-eight additional rooms to help accommodate the larger class of State Students.[\[21\]](#_edn21)

In the years after the Civil War, Virginia’s public education system finally emerged and evolved. Those changes included the scholarship for State Students at the University.[\[22\]](#_edn22) In 1870, a full-blown system of public education was created, with elementary and secondary schools. The system at birth was segregated, with white schools engaging in college preparation and Black schools limited to elementary and industrial education. 

As well, the original guidelines for selecting the State Students faded away as the Board of Visitors was given full discretion in making the selections in 1872.[\[23\]](#_edn23)  Yet Jefferson’s vision for public education was never lost. The terms of the scholarship were eventually expanded in 1876 to give all eligible students from the state of Virginia the opportunity to receive a free education at the University of Virginia.[\[24\]](#_edn24)
