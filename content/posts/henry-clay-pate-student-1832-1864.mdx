UVA student Henry Clay Pate was born April 21, 1832, in Bedford County, Virginia. His father, Edmund Pate, was a veteran of the War of 1812 and slaveholder who was committed to education as the path to self-improvement. In 1848, following his father's wishes, Pate enrolled at University of Virginia as a law student. Pate received a State Student scholarship, a then new program that provided significant tuition support to one student from each state senatorial district (who was chosen by a process localized in each district). 

Pate would spend only two years studying law at the school before withdrawing without completing a degree. After leaving UVA in 1850, Pate moved to Cincinnati, Ohio, where he worked as a journalist. Pate self-published a book in 1852 that could be described as dripping with delusional narcissism. HIs book,  *The American Vade Mecum: Or, The Companion Of Youth, And Guide To College: A Book Of Miscellanies,* was both a celebration of himself and a chance to air grievances. In it, he criticized UVA for encouraging the idea Virginia was the mother of Presidents and the cradle of the republic: "They, indeed, are the very princes of freemen; breathing, as they do, the pure breezes of their own blue mountains, and daily learning lessons of liberty and independence from the wild bird that soars in unobstructed flight and proud defiance about the towering summit of the Peaks of Otter." It is likely that Pate had felt humiliated as a State Student. He was forced to live in a separate set of dormitories designated for students with financial need and surely experienced the sting of being patronized by wealthier students whose families paid for their studies. 

Wherever Pate went, his restlessness and his supreme belief in his own greatness seemed to characterize all that he did. A colleague described Pate in Cincinnati: "Some years, four or five years perhaps, ago, the door of a lodging room, in the third story of an obscure building of this city, was labeled with startling letters 'H. CLAY PATE, AUTHOR!' Behind this door were the quarters of a slight young man who had thrust distinction upon himself, by publishing at his own expense, a book! The frontispiece thereof was a wood cut of the "author," with a facsimile of the autograph of that illustrious individual. .... It was made up of several mournful attempts at 'tales,' and a mass of essays perpetrated at college, introduced by a prodigious exhortation to all young men of the country, to go straightway and take a college course at the institution of the workmanship of which the 'author' was a blossoming specimen.-- Would that we had kept our copy of the volume..." (Quindaro Chidowan, February 13, 1858).

When sectional crisis emerged in the Kansas territory with bloody battles between pro-slavery and abolitionist/free soil factions, Pate found his next mission in life. He moved to Kansas City, Missouri, and established the *Westport Border Star*, a rabidly pro-slavery newspaper. By 1855, he became a Justice of the Peace there. He participated in the 1856 pro-slavery sacking of Lawrence, a Kansas town established by anti-slavery settlers. Days later, after news of John Brown's Pottawatomie Massacre of pro-slavery settlers, Pate formed his own brigade of thirty men who then set out to track Brown down. They successfully captured to members of Brown's party, John Brown's sons Jason and John Jr. 

John Brown soon retaliated. On June 2, 1856, Brown's men, greatly outnumbered, attacked Pate's group in an incident that became known as the Battle of Black Jack. Pate's forces were routed and Pate surrendered to Brown unconditionally after over three hours of fighting. Self-emancipated former enslaved man and abolitionist leader Frederick Douglass, writing in his revised memoir, *The Life and Times of Frederick Douglass, *effectively eulogizing John Brown, described the battle well: “With only eight men he met, fought, whipped, and captured Henry Clay Pate with twenty-five well-armed and well-mounted men. In this battle he selected his ground so wisely, handled his men so skillfully, and attacked his enemies so vigorously, that they could neither run nor fight, and were therefore compelled to surrender to a force less than one-third their own.” Pate, when later asked about the events of 1856, reportedly said: “I went to take Old Brown, and Old Brown took me.”

After being told to leave Kansas by pro-slavery leaders, Pate settled in Parksburg, Virginia (now West Virginia). In late 1859, after John Brown's failed attempt to seize the federal armory at Harper's Ferry, Virginia, Pate went to Charlestown to witness Brown's hanging on December 2. Five days later, Pate gave a speech at the Cooper Union in New York City, most of which was dedicated to lauding himself as a pro-slavery hero or to denigrating Brown. 

With the outbreak of Civil War in 1861, Pate was unsurprisingly a rabid supporter of the Confederate cause. In Parksburg, he raised his own company. His unit was deployed in western Virginia and he was displaced as the unit's leader. He was forced to serve under Thomas L. Rosser (who had been born in nearby Campbell County, Virginia, and would move to Charlottesville after buying a local plantation in 1886). Rosser quickly developed a quite low opinion of Pate, remarking that he “became non-cooperative and really an obstruction to harmony and discipline, and finally I had to arrest him and for disobedience of orders and conduct prejudicial to good order and military discipline in the regiment.” (William J. Miller, *Decision at Tom's Brook*, p. 342). Ultimately, the court martial was not successful and Pate was exonerated. 

He would go on to serve as the Lieutenant Colonel of the 5th Virginia Cavalry, serving under General J.E.B. Stuart, and is even said to have designed a new type of revolving cannon that carried five shots. At the Battle of the Yellow Tavern in May 1864, General Stuart told Pate to hold his position until reinforcements arrived. In the second wave of attack by U.S. forces, Pate was killed in action. He was thirty-two years old. Pate, although not a graduate of UVA, has long been considered an alumnus. He was among those memorialized on plaques at the UVA Rotunda dedicated those alumni who fought for the Confederacy (those plaques were removed from the Rotunda after the white nationalist violence of August 11 and 12, 2017). 

For more on Henry Clay Pate, see Peter S. Carmichael, *The Last Generation: Young Virginians in Peace, War, and Reunion.* Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 2009.
