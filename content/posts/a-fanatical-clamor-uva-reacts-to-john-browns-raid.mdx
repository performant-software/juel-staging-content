Faculty Chairman Socrates Maupin noted in October 1859 the unauthorized absences of two students, Holmes Conrad and Geo Bedinger, following John Brown's raid at Harper's Ferry. Maupin stated that "having on the 17th received such information of the occurrences at Harpers Ferry on the 16th as to excite alarm for the safety of their friends residing in the vicinity, left hurriedly on the morning of the 18th for their homes."1 Determining their justification satisfactory, Maupin excused their absences.

On October 16th, abolitionist John Brown and a small group had seized the federal armory at Harper's Ferry. Their plan had been to collect weapons from the arsenal, arm the enslaved who would flock to their banner from far and wide, and overthrow slavery in the United States.. They successfully in occupied the arsenal, but no army of enslaved people self-emancipating appeared, and John Brown did not have a clear plan for what to do next. Virginia authorities in the area sounded the alarm bells, contacted the federal government, and soon he United States Marines—led by Colonel Robert E. Lee—arrived. They launched an assault on Brown's small group occupying the arsenal and took all surviving raiders prisoner just a day later. Across the slave states, a panic ensured. Brown's attempt to use arms to overthrow slavery seemed to confirm pro-slavery apologists worst fears. Virginia wasted no time. On November 2nd, just two weeks after the raid, Brown was found guilty of treason and murder by the Commonwealth of Virginia and was sentenced to be hanged on December 2. 

In Charlottesville, John Brown's raid did not only rattle students with close connections to Harper's Ferry, but rather ignited a panic across the city. One historian described the moment: "Brown's attempted insurrection permanently changed the tone at Virginia universities, militia organizations, and debating societies. Young people started to encourage any sign of Virginian's preparing themselves for what appeared to be an inevitable conflict."2 

On November 3rd, 1859, the day following Brown's conviction, UVA student Elliot Muse Healy wrote home to his brother in Urbanna, Virginia to share his account of the atmosphere at UVA following the raid. He said "the Citizens of Charlottesville at least, look upon it as a serious thing and have appointed a mass-meeting to be held to night for the purpose of increasing the military force of the Town & Country so that they may be prepared when occasion call for them."3 The impending threat of both potential uprising  of the enslaved and of an imagined abolitionist invasion motivated Charlottesville residents and students to band together in order to provide a defense for slavery and the South.

Beyond efforts to militarize, students responded to the raid by expressing their enmity towards John Brown and abolitionism. John Minor, a law professor and the faculty's most open supporter of the Union, wrote in a letter that a "fanatical clamor" had arisen at the university in the wake of John Brown's raid. He advised that students exercise patience, and to "give time for the more quiet & order loving people to make their voices heard," but students evinced little interest in Minor's advice.4 

The agitated pro-slavery atmosphere present at UVA in that moment is emblematized by an attempt to burn John Brown in effigy, an effort that was nonetheless frowned upon by most students.5 The Jefferson Society responded to the events at Harper's Ferry by inviting Daniel Voorhees—the attorney for Brown raider John E. Cook—to address the organization. At trial, Voorhees had argued that Brown had forced his client to participate in the raid against his will. He also condemned the actions of John Brown and his fellow abolitionists during the trial. Both the attempt to burn John Brown in effigy and the Jefferson Society's invitation to Voorhees to speak at UVA highlight the frenzied atmosphere on Grounds in late 1859 and early 1860.

Perhaps even more indicative of UVA students' investment in the incident, however, was the decision of a group of students to travel to Charlestown, Virginia (near present-day Winchester, Virginia, and not far from Harper's Ferry) to witness the hanging of John Brown and allegedly, to attempt to prevent Northerners from rescuing him. In December 1859 the *Yale Literary Review* satirized UVA students' actions, writing, "the students of Virginia University having gone to Charleston to prevent a rescue…when they heard that Yale College were coming to rescue 'John Brown'" were "so frightened that they walked right home and haven't been heard from since."6 

The *Virginia University Magazine* responded in January 1860: "if gotten up by children, we would be disposed to laugh…but when they appear in the third number of the twenty-fifth volume of the *Yale Literary Magazine*, they excite our profoundest disgust."7 The article went on to defend the actions of UVA students, stating "those students, of the University of Virginia, who went to Charlestown, needed but an opportunity to prove that, though they may sometimes run, yet it is always with their faces towards the enemy."8 The blatant animosity towards Yale—and the North—foreshadowed the hostility that would ignite the Civil War in just two years.

The fanaticism that arose in the days and months following John Brown's raid generated the intensification of UVA students' long-standing support for secession and thirst for military defense of slave power.  After the raid at Harper's Ferry, "many young Virginians became receptive to the idea of a Southern nation."9 While students at the university had been sympathetic to such an idea since the turn of the decade, the raid loosened remaining allegiances to the Union and prepared young people for an impending war.10 

The *Virginia University Magazine* ended with a threat to Yale, and by extension, abolitionists and northern states: "and if, on the other hand, a *Hartford Convention* should ever assemble in our vicinity, or armed bands congregate in our midst for the purpose of wickedly invalidating northern soil and stirring up internecine strife amongst our peaceful neighbors, we'll capture the offenders and hang them as high as Haman!"11 Rhetorically imposing the fate of John Brown upon Yale—a stand-in for the abolitionist North—the *Virginia University Magazine* envisioned an idealized future of victory over what they saw as an oppressive, depraved abolitionist North. John Brown's raid not only made a future of blatant hostility between the North and the South amenable, but desirable, transforming the perspectives of southern youth and making armed sectional conflict all the more likely.
