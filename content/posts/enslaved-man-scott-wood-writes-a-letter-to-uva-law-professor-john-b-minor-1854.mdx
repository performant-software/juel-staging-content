UVA Law Professor John B. Minor's views on slavery were typical of white paternalist and Protestant Virginians' ideas about slavery and the state. In an era when the national debate over slavery repeatedly returned to the front pages of newspapers, Minor and other Virginians saw themselves as benevolent enslavers who were turning bondage into a culturally necessary but benign institution. 

Publicly, Minor sometimes questioned slavery, a judgment which many of his fellow Virginians disagreed with. Even that judgment was more about perceived abuses carried out by fire-eaters in the Deep South than about actual practices of Virginians. Despite criticism, Minor advocated for passage of Charlottesville resolutions that acknowledged the slave system's excesses as a “social and political evil,” all the while assuming that his evangelical brethren were paternally committed to caring for the bodies and souls of the people they enslaved.  

Nonetheless, Minor never divested himself of his human property and did nothing to end bondage. The benefits of unpaid labor and Minor's belief in Black inferiority outweighed his concerns about the institution.  He continued to own enslaved people and also to rent people. His practices even separated enslaved families. He used his status and wealth to help perpetuate the institution rather than suspend it. 

In a letter written to Minor dated January 2, 1854, a former enslaved servant named Scott Wood constructs a seemingly tearful farewell to his “dear master,” describing his “true gratitude” in being able to serve John Minor. Wood writes, “I dreaded the parting hour and hated to say good bye to so good and so indulgent a master for fear my eyes would be so filled with tears as to be running over.” Minor had rented Scott Wood from local Albemarle planter Benjamin Wood for the duration of 1853.

Wood's letter to Minor reveals details about Minor and evangelical Christian enslavement at UVA. Minor led Sunday “religious lessons” for Wood and others he enslaved. Sunday schooling for enslaved people at UVA, always directed by an individual enslaver (professors and hotel keepers), was never an institutional project, nor was it intended to provide a sort of civic education for the enslaved. Instead, Minor and others at UVA engaged in the practice as they sought to demonstrate their moral care for their entire “household,” including making enslaved people co-religionists who could at least experience a soul salvation after lives in human bondage.

What does Wood's letter to Minor reveal about Scott Wood, and what does it reveal about the lives of enslaved Virginians. The letter suggests sincere bond between Minor and Wood, at least in their embrace of evangelical Christianity. Wood appears truly thankful for “the many religious lessons I received at your hands” and suggests that living conditions may have been far worse back on Ben Wood's plantation. Wood's letter also hints at a shared social culture, one in which intimate interpersonal relationships could develop between white enslavers and the people they enslaved that in certain small ways may have reduced the horrors of bondage (at least for one person in such a face-to-face relationship), created informal space where Wood was less likely to experience the worst violent excesses of the slave system, and highlighted the ways enslaved people might subtly manipulate social conventions to carve out evanescent improvements in their lives. Despite all that, Scott Wood was still enslaved and still subject to the realities of life in slave Virginia. Thus, even the brotherhood of co-religious experience in Virginia, at least for Black people, came with a vast disparity in power in their relationship. Wood, while expressing deeply held religious convictions, was also demonstrating that he understood how the dominated in a slave society must perform their subjection.
