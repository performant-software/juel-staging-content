*A brief abridged version of this essay is included as part of the permanent exhibit installed in Pavilion X basement hallway.*

As with many of the early structures at UVA, the exact composition of the workcrew that built Pavilion X remains unknown. Records do suggest that some of the workers who assisted in the construction of Paviliion V & VII and Hotels, A, D, and F may also have worked on the site of Pavillion X including Davey, William, and the father and son team of Old and Young Sam. \[1]

The first occupant of Pavilion X, Professor of Medicine Robley Dunglison, at first rented enslaved laborers including two men: John and Wyatt, and one woman: Maria. Taking advantage of Thomas Jefferson's estate sale, he went on to purchase several enslaved people from Monticello, including Fanny Gillette Hern, who had been separated family by Jefferson during his presidency (1801-1808). In Washington, served as Jefferson's White House Chef. Dunglison also purchased her youngest child Bonnycastle. Dunglison would later purchased Hern's husband David at a second Monticello estate sale in 1829. By the time of the 1830 Census, Dunglison enslaved eleven people: three adults over the age of twenty-four and also eight children and young adults. The fate of these families following Dunglison's resignation in 1832 remains unclear. \[2]

Unfortunately, less information survives regarding enslaved indvididuals who served the second and third occupants of Pavilion X. Both John A.G. Davis and Henry St. George Tucker maintained separate dwellings off grounds to accommodate their large households (that of John A.G. Davis can still be found at 1201 Jefferson Street in Charlottesville, the plantation was known as “The Farm.”), so the exact divsion of labor between each space remains hazy. In all likelihood, enslaved people who worked as domestic servants moved back and forth between the plantation the pavilion with some regularity.

We do know that in 1840 Davis enslaved seventeen people: eight adults over the age of twenty-four and nine children and adolescents.

Shortly before his move to Charlottesville, Henry St. George Tucker enslaved forty-six people, including eighteen adults, and twenty-eight children, adolescents, and young adults under the age of twenty-four.\[3]

The fourth occupant of Pavillion X, John B. Minor, remained on grounds long beyond the passage of the general emancipation in 1865, serving as a Professor of Law until his death in 1895. Regardless, before 1865, Minor enslaved dozens of individuals. One of them, James, apparently resisted his ensalvement to the point where Minor concluded that he had "behaved so very badly as to make it necessary to dispose of him." Minor's response was to attempt to sell Jim away. Whether Minor could not find a buyer or had a change of heart remains unknown, but James remained in his records in the coming years.

At other moments, Minor strived for the paternalistic ideal championed by slavery's apologists. In 1856, Minor lamented that his enslaved woman named Matilda might die spiritually unprepared after developing a sudden illness. Despite his role as a Sunday school teacher for enslaved people, Minor agonized "I have no assurance, I can have but little hope, that she is prepared to die, and my conscience puts me on trial as to whether I may not have come short of my duty in instruction, exhortation, or example." We sadly do not know if Matilda survived her illness.

Minor periodically rented enslaved people away from his plantation and also from his pavilion. One, Ellen, returned to Pavillion X after enduring a horrific beating from the man who had rented her. According to an account left by Minor's daughter Mary, at least one enslaved man, Henry, fled to the U.S Army late in the Civil War.
