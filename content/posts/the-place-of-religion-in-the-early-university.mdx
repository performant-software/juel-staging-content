 Over the course of his life, Thomas Jefferson had developed very particular and thoroughly organized thoughts on both religion and the role of religion in public life. As a deist, Jefferson’s religious views were very untraditional for his time (though it was not uncommon amongst learned men of the Revolutionary generation). He demonstrated his view on religion, especially in the realm of public and private life, through his work as a politician and as a private citizen. He was committed to protecting religious liberties. That fact is seen most clearly in his authoring of the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom. He remained a firm believer in a separation between church and state (he staunchly opposed the state imposition of religion). Jefferson’s religious views also extended to how he addressed the subject and religion's role in his creation of the University of Virginia.

Jefferson designed the University of Virginia around the Rotunda, which served as a temple to reason (instead of a chapel or cathedral). The Rotunda also functioned as the the school library and had classroom space. This design was a break from many other universities at this time, many of which had a curriculum focused on training for clergy and had campuses centered on a church or cathedral. Jefferson, however, believed that a public institution such as the University of Virginia should not be tied to a particular denomination or religion. In fact, Jefferson never even incorporated a place of religious worship in his designs for the University. Instead, he symbolically placed the Rotunda in the center of the University of Virginia to illustrate the centrality of education and intellectual growth to the University.

Most of the people who lived, worked, and studied at UVA after 1826 did not share Jefferson's religious views. They were largely raised in mainline Protestant faiths and remained committed to their sectarian brand of Christianity. After Jefferson's death, there were almost immediate moves to more visibly and powerfully introduce Christian faith practices into the daily life of the University. They created a rotating Protestant chaplaincy in the 1830s, held services in Pavilion VII, built a parsonage just south and west of the Academical Village in the 1850s, and even designed right before the Civil War a large cathedral intended to close off the south end of the lawn. The onset of war put an end to the catherdral plan and it was not until the late nineteenth century that an actual University Chapel was completed, almost sixty years after Jefferson’s death. The purity of the separation of church and state featured in Jefferson’s University of Virginia proved to be complicated by the reality that after his death in 1826, the future of the University would be placed in the hands of the Board of Visitors and in those of the Faculty of the University.

 According to the Minutes of the Board of Visitors, the proposed creation of a “Sunday School” held in the University and specifically in the Rotunda was discussed by the Board of Visitors in 1833. The Chairman of the Faculty did not believe that the power to decide this matter should be held by the Board of Visitors, but rather should be a matter of the Chairman and the Faculty. The Board noted: “The Chairman of the Faculty having intimated a doubt in the record of its proceedings during the present session as to the power of the Board to permit any of the Rooms in the Rotunda to be used for the purpose of holding a Sunday School therein."

The Board of Visitors ultimately conceded the power to decide the matter regarding the holding of a “Sunday School” in the Rotunda to the Chairman of the Faculty. The Board of Visitors, however,  appeared to be strongly in favor of establishing religious activity in the Rotunda and was “highly approving the objects of such a school and desiring to extend to it every proper encouragement it is therefore." The board's only hesitation regarding the change was centered on fears that operating a Sunday School in the building might damage it. They otherwise fully approved of the plan.

Maintaining Jefferson’s vision of a University separated institutionally from religion was difficult as chaplains began to become affiliated with the University. Students did attend religious services, especially in the town of Charlottesville, as is referenced in many journals and minutes of the Chairman and of the Faculty. Other ministers would push to preach at the University itself. In 1837, Reverend Goss, a Charlottesville minister, reached out to the Chairman of the Faculty asking for the Faculty to permit a Mr. Thomas from Richmond to preach at the University (*Chairman of the Faculty Journal*, 1837). The response of the Chairman is complicated but interesting in light of the University’s mixed institutional interactions with religion. The Chairman denied the request and wrote “I today replied to his letter, & declined to grant the permission asked, on two grounds; first, because, to avoid such application from persons of whom we know nothing, or to whom it would not be proper to extend the indulgence, I have adopted as a rule for my own government whilst Chairman, to grant authority, to preach here to none but those who are invited by the Chaplain or myself, & in no case to give such authority upon application; and secondly, because it is not proper to allow the University to be made the arena of religious controversy, which might, & probably would, be the consequence of granting Mr. Thomas’s request” (*Chairman of the Faculty Journal*, 1837). The controversy being referred to is unclear, yet there is the possibility that it stemmed from Mr. Thomas, who followed the Restoration movement, often pejoratively referred to as Campbellite Baptists. 

The religious culture at UVA over the course of the nineteenth century did, in some respects, stray away from a complete separation of church and state. The University remained unaffiliated with any denomination or religious faith and through the Chairman of the Faculty, the Faculty, and the Board of Visitors, sought to establish a balance between student and faculty interest in sectarian devotional practice and maintenance of institutional non-involvement in promotion of religion or religious practice at the school.
