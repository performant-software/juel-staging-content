On June 1, 1841, during the first meeting following the school year during which Professor John Davis was shot and killed, the faculty made a substantial decision that presages the University’s future honor code. In an attempt to “secure the honors of the University,” and feeling that cheating on examinations lowered the merit of the University and lessened the value of the work completed by honest students, the faculty voted to explicitly forbid it.

They resolved that any student who cheated on an examination or any student who aids another in cheating would be refused a degree or any sort of academic distinction whatsoever. This decision likely was the first step an a process of faculty rethinking how they managed and disciplined students. In the wake of the murder of professor Davis the year before, there were surely pained conversations about how to reduce student misbehavior and violence. The change after 1840 seems to have been about increasingly appealing to students' sense of themselves as men raised to mastery in the southern culture of honor. That culture was less about how we understand honor today and more about how students understood themselves and how their reputations as good men were something they would fight intensely to protect. 

The faculty would follow up by imposing an actual honor code on students in 1842. These two decisions, combined with ending the uniform law, had some impact upon student rioting and violence.
