Though the University of Virginia represented Thomas Jefferson’s architectural and educational vision, he did not build it on his own.  The University may never have been chartered, let alone survive to the present, without the assistance of several important people. One of Jefferson's most important allies in creating the university was Fluvanna County planter John Hartwell Cocke.  Cocke graduated from the College of William and Mary in 1798.  In Fluvanna County in 1809, he purchased Bremo Plantation on the banks of the James River. During the War of 1812, he served as a general.  He served on the University’s Board of Visitors from its first year until 1852, and played a critical role in the creating the school. 

Letters to and from John Hartwell Cocke reveal so much detail about the less glamorous aspects of creating a university—from the type of roofing for buildings to hiring contractors to renting enslaved laborers to build dormitories. In one letter, Proctor Arthur S. Brockenbrough discusses whether to use iron for the roof of the Observatory, as opposed to wood and tin, and later discusses the amount of stone needed for the Rotunda steps —stone that would be cut locally by Black stone masons enslaved by Cocke and rented to UVA during construction.  Many of the people who involved in the construction of the University had also worked on building Monticello and other plantations, like Cocke's Bremo. John Neilson, an Irish joiner who was a UVA construction contracctor, also worked on both Monticello and Bremo.

A letter from Cocke’s son, Philip St. George Cocke, illustrates the ways in which being a student in the first session of UVA shares key similarities with the experiences of being a student today.  Philip discussed the classes he planned to take, mentioned that he would have extra work to catch up to other students who had arrived before him, and put off taking a particular class until he had more time to devote to it.  He also gave his preliminary judgments on the other students, criticizing the fact that, “the greater part of them are doing nothing for the promotion of their educations, but on the contrary are doing something worse than throwing away their time.”  Decades later, Philip St. George Cocke would serve as a Confederate General in the Civil War.

One aspect of UVA that was different in the 1820s was the uniform rule, which proved to be a point of contention between students and faculty until its repeal in the 1840s.  In 1828, however, the rule was just being established, and Board Secretary Nicholas Trist outlined the various options in a letter to Cocke: “students are very anxious to have fixed on immediately, see that the warm weather is nigh at hand.”  Trist discusses fabric options, including bombazine (a silk-mix fabric common in the 19th century), bombazet (a type of thin wool fabric), and silk. He noted that it seemed the students wanted silk, despite its expense, and rejected the other options, complaining, “of the heaviness of bombazet.”  Trist has other suspicions for their objection: “I imagine that the true reason is that the others are the handsomest.” Trist's passing comment more than hints at the reality—UVA students, raised to mastery on plantations and born into southern honor culture, demanded to wear clothing appropriate to their stations as leaders of men and bristled at anything that might make them look or feel inferior. 

Cocke had a number of notable correspondents outside of the University sphere as well.  One of the more well known was Charles Fenton Mercer, one of the founders of the American Colonization Society (ACS). Cocke since a conversion to evangelical Christianity circa 1815 had Cocke become deeply interested in improving slavery through providing basic Christian education for enslaved people while also preparing them for a freedom that only began with deportation to Africa. Cocke acted as president of the Albemarle County Chapter of the ACS.  Mercer’s letter was written in the wake of John Quincy Adams’ inauguration as President in 1825, an event that Mercer watched as “a disappointed spectator.”  According to Mercer, Adams was apparently “no friend of the colonization society.”

In Mercer’s eyes, the removal of “the colored race from \[Virginia’s] bosom,” was the most important goal for improving the country, and a “greater obligation to justice and humanity.” It is quite likely that Cocke agreed. A similar sentiment is echoed in a letter from H.B. Montague, another member of the society - Montague saw the solution of colonization as, “a spiritual blessing to that unfortunate race."  Mercer though sharing in support for the ACS with Cocke, likely parted ways with Cocke as he thought about slavery. Mercer also opposed the slave trade, considering it “piracy,” and sought to abolish slavery altogether, seeing it as the cause of “the calamities of both continents.” Cocke bought, sold, and rented enslaved people as part of his daily business, evincing no interest in abolishing slavery. 

Many other Virginians supported Colonization (deportation) as a condition for Black freedom, including John Tyler, James Monroe, and even Thomas Jefferson, who first proposed colonization in his 1783 version of “Notes on the State of Virginia.”  Most Virginians saw removal of former enslaved people not as a way to end slavery but as a way to make it more efficient and less violent. Jefferson believed that emancipation would not have popular support unless former enslaved people were removed from the United States. Writing in 1814, Jefferson argued “I have seen no proposition so expedient . . . as that of emancipation of those \[slaves] born after a given day, and of their education and expatriation at a proper age.”  Several attempted revolutions by enslaved people in the first few years of the nineteenth century (Gabriel's Rebellion in 1800, Nat Turner's Revolt in 1831, and others) only increased the desires of many white Virginians to find a place abroad to send enslaved people whom they freed. Following England's establishment of Sierra Leone in the late eighteenth century as a colony where enslaved people could be freed and removed from England and its colonies, the American Colonization Society founded the Liberia colony in 1822 as a destination for free Black Americans (whether already free or manumitted with the express condition that they leave the United States). 

Cocke, Jefferson, and many other Virginians held similar views of slavery.  Both Cocke and Jefferson enslaved people while understanding that slavery represented a great evil of society.  As an evangelical protestant, Cocke wrestled with the moral dimension of slavery, and believed that the institution undermined society and the family unit. However, he also believed that freeing Black Virginians and making them “equal” with their former masters would not work (like Jefferson, he had a generally dim racist imagination about the Black capacity for freedom). Cocke and other ACS supporters thought that the creation of a free colony in Africa was the solution.  

Cocke purchased Hopewell plantation in Alabama that was ostensibly established as a place where the people he enslaved could in theory work towards education and passage to Africa. He sent 37 enslaved people there in 1840 and by 1860, more than seventey enslaved people labored there. His “school” preparing enslaved people for freedom and deportation ultimately sent only fourteen “graduates” to freedom in Liberia. The reality was that Cocke remained more interested in improving the efficiency of the slave system than in ending his participation in human bondage. His purchase of plantation lands in Alabama coincided with a spectacular cotton production boom across the Deep South that made him much wealthier. 

Despite their supposed disdain for human bondage, both Cocke and Jefferson participated in and benefitted from it for their entire adult lives. Even Cocke, much more visibly committed to colonization and with it the idea that slavery could be improved, ran plantation concerns that tell a different story. Cocke's list of regulations for the overseer at Bremo shows that he still allowed the brutality of slavery to continue: “For a violation of either of the last rules, after once admonishing such conection by whipping, as the overseer may find necessary, or stopping the meat rations of the offender, for the week.” Slavery's improvements would still come with the threat and reality of the lash. 

Though Cocke and Jefferson achieved great success in building the University, their participation in slavery shows they were still deeply flawed people and contributed to the complicated history of UVA.
