The 1830s proved to be a particularly challenging time, representing perhaps the nadir for faculty-student relations at the University of Virginia.  Unruly students refused to comply with faculty rules demanding high standards of conduct. Student disturbances included noisy parties, drunkenness, attacks upon professors (throwing rocks and bricks at them, horsewhipping, and worse) and routine assaults of enslaved people on Grounds. 

Not all student protests fit that mold, however. For example, on November 3, 1838, Faculty Chairman Gessner Harrison reported that a group of students sought to bring a traveling preacher on Grounds. The students claimed that the minister, Alexander P. Campbell, ought to be allowed to deliver a sermon in the Rotunda since he would preach only on "the evidences of Christianity" without introducing "sectarian issues." The faculty, likely in the interest of preserving UVA's non-sectarian reputation (Thomas Jefferson had established UVA as a fully secular institution) refused to allow the minister to speak in the Rotunda, stating that to do so would set an "inconvenient precedent." 

Some days later, those same students pinned a "notice" on the Rotunda door, calling a meeting of to remonstrate against the faculty's decision to bar Campbell from preaching. The faculty, seeing the notice, then even unsuccessfully sought to prevent the student meeting from happening. On November 6, three days after the initial request, the Rotunda's public lecture hall became the site of an excited student gathering. 

The students produced a set of "resolutions condemning the course taken by the Faculty." The next day, another group of students "of good standing" who opposed the resolutions asked permission to meet in the Rotunda's public rooms to "prevent any evil consequences from the intended publication of the resolutions" in question. 

In the midst of a decade marked by seemingly continuous and often outrageous student misbehavior and by rioting and mob action, these religious dissidents, and the faculty and students who responded to them, stand out as a rare instance of organized, non-violent student protest of faculty decisions.
