Robley Dunglison (January 4, 1798–April 1, 1869) was a young English medical doctor and educator and the first full-time professor of medicine in the United States when he took a professorship at the University of Virginia in 1825. Already a London doctor and scholar who specialized in obstetrics, he was a rising star in England when he was approached by UVA emissary Francis Walker Gilmer in early fall 1824. Gilmer offered Dunglison the anatomy and medicine professorship at the not-yet-open University of Virginia. Dunglison signed the contract on September 28, 1824. A week later, he married Harriet Leadam.

Dunglison and his new wife left London for Plymouth on October 27, 1824, along with Thomas Key, Charles Bonnycastle, and Bonnycastle’s wife. They had boarded the Competitor several days later, but the ship remained anchored in Plymouth Harbor as late as December 5. Dunglison in his memoirs indicated they “were detained in the British Channel until the 16th of December.”¹ 

After over six weeks at sea, they arrived in Norfolk on February 10, where they would remain until February 15, when the next packet boat departed for Richmond. Packet boat trips from Norfolk to Richmond typically took about fifteen hours, so the Dunglisons arrived in Richmond late February 15.² Dunglison remembered they “remained for a short time to lay in a stock of articles for commencing housekeeping at the University, as we were warned that but little choice of material was to be found at Charlottesville.”³ BoV member Joseph Carrington Cabell visited Dunglison and the other professors on February 17, while they were still in Richmond. One evening between February 16 and Dunglison’s departure from the city, “a large number of the citizens of the town were invited to meet them at a formal reception.” Writing on February 27, Maria Randolph remembered the affair as quite the scene. She wrote that “the grave seemed to have given up the dead, for there cam ladies whom I have never heard of being out before for years to see the English people.” The soiree included music, dancing, and “the boisterous mirth of the whole company.”⁴

Jefferson wrote to John Pickering on February 20, noting that “Our European Professors, five in number, are at length arrived” in Virginia.⁵ Two, George Blaettermann and George Long, had made it to Charlottesville well before that date. Dunglison, along with Bonnycastle and Key, were still in Richmond at that point. Two days later, on February 22, Jefferson wrote to James Madison letting him know about the newly scheduled March 4 UVA Board of Visitors meeting and commenting that “Our newly arrived Professors will come up in the stage of the day after tomorrow. Mr. Cabell writes me that they were much pleased with them in Richmd.”⁶ The same day, Jefferson also wrote to board member James Breckenridge about the upcoming meeting. He told Breckenridge “2 of our British Professors \[Long and Blaetterman\] have been here some time, and we like them much. 3 others \[Dunglison, Bonnycastle, and Key\] will be here the day after tomorrow & mr Cabell writes me that they were much liked in Richmd.”⁷

Thus, the professors boarded a stagecoach for Charlottesville on Thursday, February 24. The Central Hotel’s records to February 24 confirm that the professors had not yet arrived at UVA.⁸ The stage from Richmond to Charlottesville departed Sundays, Tuesdays, and Thursdays at 4:00 a.m., and took at least 24 hours to complete. Departing Richmond on February 24, they endured a slow and difficult trip that took two full days. Dunglison, Bonnycastle, and Key arrived in Charlottesville late on February 25, after the stage stopped just short of Charlottesville on the banks of  Moore’s Creek, which was at flood stage thanks to the recent heavy rain. Dunglison and the other professors had to wade repeatedly through the water, carrying all their belongings and their wives to the Charlottesville side. They surely arrived wet and weary late that evening at the Central Hotel. Jefferson personally visited Dunglison and the other professors at the hotel on February 26. It would be another day before any of them actually made it to the university’s grounds. They would move into the Academical Village on February 27, and just ten days later, the university opened on March 7. 

The journey from England to Virginia had been long and difficult, the journey from the coast to Charlottesville has also been quite challenging. Beginning a new life at UVA proved to be filled with its own challenges. Dunglison in his memoirs complained about the difficulty of finding good domestic help in Albemarle County.⁹ In early April 1825, just over a month after arriving at UVA, Dunglison asked UVA Bursar Alexander Garrett if he “knew where he could purchase a dining room servant.” Garrett convinced Dunglison to rent an enslaved boy from John Hartwell Cocke for fifty dollars a year. Dunglison agreed and urged Cocke to “send him up with the least possible delay.”¹⁰

In January 1827, apparently still lacking the domestic servants he desired, Dunglison attended one of Thomas Jefferson’s estate sales, where he purchased enslaved cook Fanny Gillette Hern (1788–1829+) and her children Indridge (1819–1862+) and Bonnycastle (1826–1829+). Two years later, in January 1829, Dunglison again purchased an enslaved man, David Hern (1784–1829+), who was Fanny’s husband. By February 1829, Dunglison enslaved at least four people. Another account indicates that Dunglison also “bought a negro for his own household service…two hundred and forty dollars — the negro was probably under age.”¹¹ By 1830, living in Pavilion X, his household included eleven enslaved people, some of whom had been purchased at Thomas Jefferson’s estate sales. 

Dunglison taught at UVA until 1833. He was the author of several medical texts, and after the publication of Human Physiology in 1832, became known as the “Father of American Physiology.” While at UVA, he also served as private physician to three U.S. presidents: Thomas Jefferson, James Madison, and James Monroe. As Jefferson’s personal physician, Dunglison attended to him during Jefferson’s last days in summer 1826. In 1827, his professorship was expanded to include physiology, medicine, obstetrics, and medical jurisprudence.

During his time in Charlottesville, Dunglison oversaw the early expansion of the medical program—this included the opening of the Anatomical Theater in 1827 and the simultaneous hiring of a new faculty member, the demonstrator of anatomy, Thomas Johnson, whose job was to perform dissections for the students in the new building. Dunglison oversaw medical training, built out a collection of anatomical specimens, and handled all classroom lecturing. In 1828, Dunglison expanded Thomas Johnson’s role to include anatomy and surgery and also promoted him from demonstrator to professor. That was likely in response to Dunglison’s academic leadership. In 1826 and again from 1828 to 1830, Dunglison served as the chairman of the faculty. His wife gave birth to their first four children while at UVA. In Dunglison’s last year at UVA, the school also opened a student dissecting hall, a small brick building near the Anatomical Theater. During his six years at UVA, Dunglison taught James Lawrence Cabell, who would go on to teach medicine at UVA for decades.

Dunglison left UVA in 1833 to chair materia medica, therapeutics, hygiene, and medical jurisprudence at the University of Maryland School of Medicine in Baltimore. In 1836, after three years in Maryland, he took another professorship at Jefferson Medical College in Philadelphia, where he would work for over thirty years (1836–1868). He became a U.S. citizen in 1838.
