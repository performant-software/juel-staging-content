Charles Scott Venable was a mathematician, Confederate staff officer, and longtime professor at the University of Virginia, where he taught mathematics from 1865 until his retirement in 1896. A former aide-de-camp to Robert E. Lee, Venable used his postwar academic career to help expand UVA's offerings in the applied sciences while also working actively to memorialize the Confederacy and promote the Lost Cause narrative of the Civil War.

## Early Years and Education

Venable was born April 19, 1827, at Longwood, his family's plantation in Farmville, Virginia. His father, Nathaniel E. Venable, was a merchant, planter, legislator, and War of 1812 officer; the Longwood property reportedly came into his possession through his uncle Abram Venable, the first president of the Bank of Virginia. The family, who were Presbyterians, had deep ties to Hampden-Sydney College: Venable's great-grandfather, also named Nathaniel, had studied at the College of William and Mary, been raised at the family's Slate Hill plantation, and helped found Hampden-Sydney as a mathematician in his own right.

Venable followed this path into Hampden-Sydney, enrolling in September 1839 and graduating in June 1842. He remained an additional year for further study before being appointed tutor in mathematics, a post he held until June 1845, when he left to continue his education at the University of Virginia. At UVA he was reportedly a member of the Philanthropic Literary Society and the Beta Theta Pi fraternity, and he studied mathematics, ancient languages, and junior law under professors including Edward Courtenay, William H. McGuffey, Gessner Harrison, and John B. Minor.

## Early Academic Career

In the summer of 1846, Hampden-Sydney's board elected Venable, then just finishing his studies, to its vacant mathematics professorship. He took a leave of absence in 1847–1848 to return to UVA, where he studied further under Courtenay while also taking courses in chemistry and natural philosophy from the Rogers brothers and modern languages from Schele de Vere. He resumed teaching at Hampden-Sydney in the fall of 1848 and remained there until 1856, taking a second leave in the fall of 1852 to pursue additional study in Germany before returning to Virginia in December 1853.

Venable left Hampden-Sydney in January 1856 for a professorship in natural philosophy at the University of Georgia, but his tenure there lasted only a year amid what a later biographical sketch described only as unspecified "questions of authority" between the faculty and trustees. Within months of his resignation, the University of South Carolina offered him a professorship in mathematics and astronomy, a position he took up in 1857 and held for three years, until the outbreak of the Civil War.

The 1860 census recorded Venable living in Columbia, South Carolina, where he enslaved one person.

## Civil War Service

Following South Carolina's secession, Venable enlisted as a second lieutenant in the Congaree Rifles and was present at the firing on Fort Sumter. He went on to serve in a series of roles over the course of the war: as a member of the Governor's Guards, he fought as a private at the First Battle of Manassas; he subsequently served as a volunteer aide to Wade Hampton, as a lieutenant of artillery in New Orleans, and as captain and adjutant to General M. L. Smith during the defense of Vicksburg, Mississippi.

In March 1862, when Robert E. Lee was named commander of Confederate forces and military advisor to the Confederate president, Lee selected Venable as one of four aides-de-camp, with the rank of major. Venable served on Lee's staff for the remainder of the war, until the surrender at Appomattox in 1865.

## University of Virginia Career

At the war's end, UVA's mathematics chair, Albert Taylor Bledsoe, remained in Europe, reluctant to return to the United States for fear of imprisonment for his wartime service to the Confederacy. On August 18, 1865, the university offered the vacant professorship to Venable, who accepted and would remain on the faculty for more than three decades.

Venable used his position to advocate for the growth of the applied sciences at the university. Within two years of his arrival, in 1867, UVA established separate departments of applied chemistry and applied mathematics. In 1870 he was elected chairman of the faculty—at the time, the university's de facto chief administrative officer—a position he held for three years. He later lobbied successfully for the creation of a department of practical astronomy and played a central role in raising funds for what became Brooks Hall. He also pushed to broaden the university's science curriculum further, adding instruction in agriculture, natural history, and geology.

Throughout his UVA career, Venable remained active in commemorating the Confederacy and promoting the Lost Cause interpretation of the war. In 1879 he published an account of Robert E. Lee's leadership during the Wilderness Campaign, and roughly a decade later he wrote a memoir of his own wartime service, which was never published.

## Later Life and Death

Venable retired from teaching at the University of Virginia in 1896. He died in Charlottesville in 1900.
