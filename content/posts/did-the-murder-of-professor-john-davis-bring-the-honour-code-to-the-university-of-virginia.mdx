*\*Originally submitted as a term paper for HIUS 3281: History of Virginia, 1600-1865, for Prof. George Gilliam\** 

The murder of Professor John A.G. Davis at the hands of a student on November 12, 1840, irrevocably shaped the University of Virginia. The aftermath of the murder saw the creation of the incident’s most lasting legacy: the UVA Honor Code, a story that lives on today in memory as more myth than history.

 From 1825 to 1840, a number of factors contributed to heightened tensions between students and faculty during the university's early years Jefferson’s vision of student self-government, students’ subsequent resistance to authority, and the notion of southern ‘honor’ all played roles in creating a tension-filled climate. Several key events created animosity among students towards the faculty: institutional decisions to enforce a uniform law, to install a campus bell denoting times to rise and times to pass to different classes,  the banning of organized public speaking at the university, and the 1838 dismissal of student Henry C. Mayer. That last incident was met with student unrest and a petition movement demanding Mayer be readmitted.  Ultimately, Davis’s murder acted as a catalyst that encouraged students to cooperate more with the faculty, which led them to embrace the honor system that stemmed from the first faculty-introduced ‘honor pledge’ two years later in 1842.

Thomas Jefferson, founder of the university, was somewhat idealistic in his original plans for his university. He believed students and staff would be able to cooperate to ensure the smooth running of the university and that students would be responsive to the rules he put in place at the institution.[\[1\]](#_ftn1) He did not want to evoke fear in the students in order to enforce discipline at UVA, so he advised that their pride, ambition, and moral sensitivities alone should be appealed to by the faculty in order to ensure order at the university.[\[2\]](#_ftn2) He was also opposed to corporal punishment, and firmly supported the idea of student self-governance.[\[3\]](#_ftn3) 

As a result, a number of the early students at UVA opposed any form of rules being enforced by faculty, regardless of how trivial they seemed. In 1831, the faculty attempted to enforce a uniform for the students, in an bid to create unity between rich and poor scholars and to discourage frivolous spending or displays of wealth.[\[4\]](#_ftn4) The university relied on public funds, so the Board of Visitors wanted to try and dispel illusions among the public that Jefferson’s university was an elitist establishment. Students immediately mounted significant resistance to the law that did not begin to dissipate until the rule was relaxed in 1834. Another eight years would pass, and with that time more student protests about the uniform law, before it was repealed entirely in 1842.[\[5\]](#_ftn5) Jefferson’s vision of student self-governance may have contributed to the tensions that arose between students and staff as students tried to resist the introduction of new rules to the university.

Additionally, the role of ‘honor’ in the South before the Civil War was important. Young men were educated in the importance of being ‘honorable’ from childhood, and in the words of historian Bertram Wyatt-Brown, in southern colleges “honor was an unannounced part of the curriculum”.[\[6\]](#_ftn6) Since students tended to be younger when they attended college, the role of instilling ‘honour’ in these young men fell to the staff of the universities they attended.[\[7\]](#_ftn7) However, many rich young men used their ideas of ‘honor’ and liberty as a platform to disregard rules and regulations that the faculty attempted to enforce.[\[8\]](#_ftn8) They saw the introduction of arbitrary rules as an infringement on their individual liberties. In some ways, that was inevitable—they had been raised to mastery in a slave society and understood that their position atop the social and political order depended upon how others viewed them. If they were esteemed as honorable, manly, and masters, then they effectively were just that. But any challenge to the public display of their mastery and manhood represented a deeply threatening and offensive affront.

As a result, students frequently petitioned the faculty of the university to express concerns and grievances about matters seemingly large and small. One of the earliest student remonstrances came in UVA's first year in 1825, when “a petition \[was] received from and signed by 78 students of the University, praying for a vacation of 10 days or more, according to the discretion of the Professors, to commence on the 4th July, the pleas assigned being the unusual length of the session and the immoderate heat of the weather."[\[9\]](#_ftn9) The decision was deemed not to fall within the jurisdiction of the faculty, and was thus handed over to the Rector, Jefferson himself. These petitions continued to be a common way for students to express their grievances regarding aspects of university life even after Jefferson’s death in 1826. 

In September 1832 the students petitioned directly against an action being taken by the faculty. The subject of their protest was the time of their meals, which the faculty was considering deferring without consulting with the students.[\[10\]](#_ftn10) The resolution to this issue was not noted in the Faculty Minutes, indicating that the issue was not considered of any great importance to the minute-taker. However, the students were clearly willing to protest their unhappiness with faculty decisions through formal means as well as through rioting, which was also common throughout the early years of the university. This practice of petitioning was part of the formalized structure of dispute in the southern culture of honor. If the petition were ignored or rejected, students might easily move to direct action. At UVA, that was generally what appears to us today to be a form of mob action.

 By 1837 petitions to the faculty were becoming more demanding. On April 1, 1837, the faculty received a petition signed by six students requesting “a recession of the resolution of the Faculty of the 8th ultimo” which had seen a student, William R. Battey, receiving a two-month suspension for consuming alcohol on university grounds.[\[11\]](#_ftn11) The petitioners promised to inform the Chairman if Battey violated the rules, and also presented a letter from Battey in which he was “promising to conform to the laws”.[\[12\]](#_ftn12) Battey was permitted to re-join the university, demonstrating that the faculty had at least some trust in the students keeping their word.[\[13\]](#_ftn13)  

However, whilst tension between students and faculty were present from the university’s founding, they were particularly heightened in the mid-1830s. Faculty and administrators were feeling increasingly threatened by the forms of student self-organization. Students reacted angrily when the Faculty, upset by the growing membership of prominent student societies, banned the delivery of public "literary addresses" and the public celebration of anniversaries in 1837.[\[14\]](#_ftn14) The Jefferson and Washington Societies were growing to be especially noteworthy, and faculty was allegedly concerned that elections to these societies were distracting students from their studies and had the potential to damage the university’s reputation. 

Again, the university’s position as a publicly-funded institution would most likely have played a role in their concern over the reputation of the establishment. Also, it is worth noting that the university was still in its infancy at the time, so there was a very real risk that it could face closure if students became too unruly. The following is an extract from the students’ response to the ban, published in *The Collegian* in June 1839:

*“It is indeed mortifying that a custom, venerable in time, and sanctioned by the happy experience of all other Colleges should be prohibited our use. We are forbidden to speak \[…] the voice of vivid eloquence must ring through our Corinthian columns no more. The effect of the present policy will be to hush up all emulation and render neglected the cultivation of elocution. Public spirit will linger in faint breathings amongst us”.[\[15\]](#_ftn15)*

There are two noteworthy aspects of this statement: the students’ focus on their relation with other colleges at the time, and their interest in preserving ‘public spirit.' Student protest was not uncommon throughout the United States during this period. The American Revolution had politicized college campuses across the country to a greater extent than they had been before the revolution.[\[16\]](#_ftn16) Young men in the 1830s had grown up hearing stories about the revolution. Those stories may have motivated students as they harbored all sorts of grievances whenever they felt the university was treating them as inferiors, attempting to “unman” them, or, as they put it, make them “slaves.” Unsurprisingly, one idea frequently debated by student ‘literary societies’ like the Jefferson Society was the issue of slavery, a particularly contentious one in the state of Virginia, and one that shaped their sense of manhood, hierarchy, and social order.[\[17\]](#_ftn17) 

Other elite colleges, including Harvard, experienced significant student protest during the period between the Revolution and the Civil War, so it is important to appreciate that the presence of student unrest was not unique to the University of Virginia. What was unique, however, was the way the faculty responded to the problem. Their response reflects both the history of early student-faculty negotiation and the fact that five of the first eight professors were from Europe, where the faculty-student relationship was much more clearly defined as a master-apprentice relationship. Thus, early faculty decisions to reign in the excesses of students were invariably meted out as orders to inferiors. That always had the inintended effect of stoking student anger and often creating yet more unrest. It would not be until the 1840s, when most faculty members were Virginia-born, that faculty began to think more creatively about how to harness the culture of honor to encourage students to choose to behave differently.  

Furthermore, during the years of unrest there was a significant example of students successfully protesting the decision of the faculty. Henry C. Mayer was accused of being a “ring leader in the riot” among students on  November 12, 1838, and was subsequently swiftly dismissed from the university.[\[18\]](#_ftn18) However, unhappy with his experience of the dismissal process, he wrote a letter to the committee appealing their decision. He claimed that his actions were carried out as a result of “recklessness and folly, but not through any ill will towards the Faculty or any of its members, or any premeditated ill design \[…] the disgrace is the principal punishment which the Faculty could inflict”.[\[19\]](#_ftn19)  He argued that the reason he had not defended himself at his disciplinary hearing was:

 *“a misapprehension of the proceedings of the Faculty in these cases. I believed, that the Faculty never acted but upon direct and positive evidence of participation in particular Acts. I knew that no such evidence could be brought against me; and on this ground, I refused to answer any questions, by means of my answers to which I might possibly disclose my participation in the riot to a greater extent than was already known to the Faculty”.[\[20\]](#_ftn20)*

Mayer effectively blamed his expulsion on the unfair practice of the Faculty, which dismissed him without evidence of his involvement in the incident provoking his expulsion. The faculty ultimately relented. Mayer was readmitted, and he was not the only student readmitted after a decision to expel.[\[21\]](#_ftn21) It is noteworthy that one of the first cases of a student appealing such a significant faculty decision came at a time when tensions between students and faculty were especially heightened.

Now, what about that fall night in 1840 when student misbehavior in the evening ended with a struggle and the shooting of professor Davis? It is important to recognize that on the night of his murder, Davis was responding to student unrest when stepped onto the Lawn from his residence in Pavilion X. 

He was the Chairman of the University at the time of his death, and his assassination was recorded in the *Proceedings of the Faculty of the University of Virginia* as follows:

*“\[Davis] has been snatched from us by the hands of a ruthless assassin, under circumstances of peculiar atrocity \[…] in the vigor of health, and in the meridian of life, was shot down before his own doorsill, in the wantonness of ruffian malice, when he had no suspicion of danger, was without the means of injury or defence, and when his only provocation was an unsuccessful attempt to discover who had disturbed his domestic peace and violated the laws of the University \[…] In a spirit of exalted piety he died forgiving his reckless murderer”.[\[22\]](#_ftn22)*

What is most interesting about Davis’s shooting is that his killer was unknown at the time of his death (Davis refusing to name his killer). Some students helped the shooter and his accomplice flee the University, but for perhaps the first time, some students actively engaged with the faculty in order to track down the offenders. This was the first time students had cooperated with staff after an act of violence had occurred at the university, rather than choosing to stand together with the offender.[\[23\]](#_ftn23) The murder of Davis shocked the student body, as some realized their acts of violence and mayhem had the potential to have deadly consequences.[\[24\]](#_ftn24) Although student violence erupted several times in the years following Davis’s death, it became less frequent. Therefore, the shooting was in some small way a turning point for the university, as it marked the moment when faculty and administrators began to think differently about how to encourage good student behavior—switching to appealing to their shared sense of manhood and honor instead of giving them orders and subjecting them to discipline. It was also perhaps the moment when some students began to choose to cooperate with their teachers without always mounting resistance.

Finally, although emphasis is placed on the role of Davis’s murder on the introduction of the first honor pledge, it was not officially introduced until almost two years later, on July 4, 1842. More importantly, it was not a student proposal, but a faculty proposal, the first to clearly attempt to appeal to students' sense of themselves as men of honor. As instituted in 1842, faculty voted that the pledge was to be written when students took exams in order to prevent cheating. The original words of the pledge were:

"*I do hereby certify on honor that I have derived no assistance during the time of this examination from any source whatever, whether oral written or in print in giving the above answers".[\[25\]](#_ftn25)*

There is no mention of Davis or student violence at any point in the *Minutes* where the introduction of the pledge was recorded.[\[26\]](#_ftn26) The Faculty Chairman's journals from that period, which until his murder in 1840 were literally written by Davis, are missing, so we may never know more about what happened in those two years. As a result, one may question whether the two incidents were truly related. Another key faculty change was also proposed on the same day as the honor code. Professors asked the Board of Visitors to also repeal the Uniform Law and the Early Rising Law, two of the most hated enactments and two that students most clearly saw as attempting to make “slaves” of them.[\[27\]](#_ftn27) Although the records are silent as to the motivations for these changes, it seems likely that it was a new class of southern-born professors who better understood the culture of honor and thus sought new ways to encourage good behavior. Subsequently, students willingly complied with the newly introduced honor pledge. 

The status of the University of Virginia as one of the country’s leading educational establishments was far from inevitable in the decades following its foundation. Student riots, protests, and rebellions jeopardized its funding, and keeping the first generations of students under control was by no means an easy task. Jefferson’s belief in a utopian system of student self-governance where faculty would have minimal involvement in maintaining order at the university was simply unfeasible. The young, wealthy, southern men who took up the first places at the university resisted any rules that could be seen as an infringement on their liberties as men born to leadership, making enforcing student discipline a particular challenge. The unprovoked murder of the professor Davis brought these tensions to a climax and may have forced both students and faculty to recognize that the current state of affairs could not continue. Although there would remain trials to overcome, the introduction of the ‘honor pledge’ marked an important step in the university’s journey to become home to “the illimitable freedom of the human mind”.[\[28\]](#_ftn28) It also marked its transition from a Jeffersonian “School of Athens” into a pro-slavery flagship state university in the state with the largest population of enslaved people in the U.S.
