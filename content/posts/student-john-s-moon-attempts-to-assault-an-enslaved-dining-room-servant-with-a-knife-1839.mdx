On September 30, 1839, a case of violent student misconduct was brought before the Faculty. According to UVA hotelkeeper Col. Ward, During meal service, Student John S. Moon threw a knife at an enslaved dining room servant. The incident was brought to the faculty's attention when Ward complained about the disruptive behavior and potential damage to his human property. The Faculty called Moon to appear before them. During that interview, Moon claimed that he had thrown the knife at the enslaved man because the enslaved man was being inattentive and neglected him. Moon explained that he threw the knife at the enslaved servant because “he had spoken to him frequently and was only answered by laughing.” Moon argued that the supposed insolence of the enslaved man was the spark that necessitated the attempted knife attack. 

Hotelkeeper Ward also spoke with the faculty. He argued that Mr. Moon had behaved poorly while also defending the enslaved dining room servant, whom Ward described as normally attentive and certainly “not insolent.” Ward's testimony ultimately likely revealed what precipitated the attempted knifing of the enslaved dining room servant. Ward stated “it was Mr. Moon’s desire to have his \[the enslaved dining room servant's] exclusive attendance.” Thus, John S. Moon had demanded complete the complete subservience and attention of an enslaved man who was likely charged by the hotelkeeper with attending to many diners at that meal. Moon, raised to be a master of men in a slave society, was behaving accordingly (at least in his mind) for a man of his station. 

Ultimately, the faculty suspended Mr. Moon for a month and required to serve the suspension back at his father’s home. 

Although in this instance, no one was physically harmed, the incident sheds light on how seemingly routine dining hall service often represented for the enslaved situations charged with potential violence.
