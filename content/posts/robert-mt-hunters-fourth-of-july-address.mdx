On July 4th, 1839, UVA alumnus Robert Mercer Taliaferro Hunter (1829) returned to his alma mater to give a speech celebrating American independence and outlining what he thought were the wellsprings of the American democratic republic.  At the time, Hunter was a U.S. Congressman representing Virginia's 9th District. Months later in 1839, he would later rise Speaker of the House. Eight years later, he would represent Virginia in the United States Senate, a position he would hold until he resigned to join the Confederacy in 1861.   He also on the UVA the Board of Visitors from 1845-1851.  

Hunter opened his speech by discussing the contributions of the Athenians, making several mentions in particular of Pericles, “who changed the entire spirit of their institutions.”  Pericles was a major proponent of Athenian democracy, and led the Delian League.  The Delian League was originally based in Delos, where Pericles “seized the common treasure.”  In the midst of discussing the democratic and cultural achievements of the Athenians, Hunter quotes “Elegy Written in a Country Courtyard,” published by Thomas Gray in 1751: "Hands that the rod of empire might have swayed, Then waked to ecstasy the living lyre.”

Continuing with the theme of Athenian greatness, Hunter referenced a painting of Alexander the Great by Apelles of Kos.  He uses this reference to emphasize the lasting impact Athens and Greek civilization had on the world, asserting that although the “colors of Apelles have faded… the radiant forms of Athenian genius must endure.”

 Hunter then moved on to explain the rise of republican institutions in America.  He mentioned two groups of people who fled persecution in the Old World, the Cavaliers and the Round Heads, who brought with them, “a love of adventure and a spirit of independence.”  Roundheads were supporters of a constitutional monarchy and Parliament during the English Civil War in the 17th century.  Many Roundheads were Puritans, who later settled in New England where, “the republican spirit \[was] self-educated in the wilderness, \[and] reached a high degree of cultivation.”  Cavaliers were the opponents of Roundheads, and supported the King and an absolute monarchy. The Cavaliers would later lead English settler colonies to the south, including in Virginia.

Honoring the spirit of the American Founding Fathers, Huntere quoted Thomas Moore’s oriental romance, “Lalla Rookh.”  That poem is about the daughter of an emperor engaged to a king of the Persian state Bukhara.  While journeying to meet him, a poet regales her, and the work is comprised mainly of stories told by him to Lalla.  Hunter homed in on “The Veiled Prophet of Khorassan,” reciting: "That high and lofty name, The light, the landmark on the cliffs of fame," words spoken by the hero Azim. Hunter's goal here was to assert that the way to free a man from the “sloth” of  life is by teaching him virtue that will allow his name to be famous after his death.

Hunter, a pro-slavery ideologue who would be among the first Virginians to eagerly join the newly formed Confederate government in March 1861, naturally understood slavery to be a foundational element necessary for the success of his imagined American republic. To bring that point home, he quoted one of Roman poet Horace's odes in defense of human bondage. Hunter argued that slavery allowd Black people to live in a country where they “subsist with most comfort and \[are] best fitted for occupation.”  Hunter was not done, it was not enough, in his estimation, to assert that enslaved people were better off in bondage in Virginia. He understood that assertion as axiomatic. Hunter thought that to argue with that sentiment, would be as useful as trying to reason with the “caput insanabile tribus Anticyris." That phrase translates as “a person incurable by three Anticyrae." The Anticyrae were Greek islands and ports south of Corinth known for black and white hellebore, where travelers from across the Mediterranean would come to seek Hellebore preprations to treat madness and a host of other ailments. In other words, to argue that slavery was not the foundation on which the independent American republic was built and thrived upon, was to mark oneself as an incurably insane person.  

Hunter's expository analysis of the Western canon for additional defenses of slavery. He would reference William Shakespeare's *Henry IV, reciting *“Let one spirit of the first born Cain…Reign in all bosoms, that each heart being set on bloody courses, the rude scene may end, and darkness be the burier of the dead." Hunter may have been attempting to discourage the use of force as a means to end slavery, and also suggesting that any attempt to end slavery would upend the very social hierarchy the South was built upon. He was also likely hinting, through this passage's reference to Cain, what was widely understood in slave states as the “curse of Ham,” an intentional grotesque distortion of  Genesis 9: 18-29 that falsely claimed that Ham (Cain's son) was “turned black” and that Africans were Ham's descendants destined by God for perpetual servitude.

At one point in his oratory, Hunter turned to the reputation of Virginia, claiming it was not as prestigious a state as it once had been.  He urged the “sons” of Virginia to rally around their home state and restore it to its former political glory.  He compared the case of Virginia to that of Randolph at Bannockburn, stating, “that the rose had fallen from her chaplet.”  The reference here was to the Battle of Bannockburn, a victory in the First War for Scottish Independence in 1314.  Thomas Randolph was a commander of Scottish troops; at one point in the battle, Randolph miscalculated and let English troops pass by where his army was supposed to be holding them back.  Legend has it that Robert the Bruce, King of Scots, exclaimed to Randolph, “A rose has fallen from your chaplet, Randolph!”  Another commander offered to assist Randolph, but Bruce insisted Randolph fix his mistake for himself, as Virginia needed to fix its mistakes and regain respect in the United States.

Hunter, ever the pro-slavery apologist, also found time praise to the editor of the *Farmers Register*, Edmund Ruffin, whom he says “has rendered \[Virginia] more service than most, or perhaps, all of its politicians in the last twenty years,”.  He compared Ruffin to “a Sinclair or a Coke,” referring to Scottish politician and agricultural scientist Sir John Sinclair and Welsh politician Thomas Coke credited with helping launch the English agricultural revolution. Here, Hunter was imagining southern slave states and enslavers as engines of efficiency and modernity. It was thus slavery that would lead the United States from the celebration of its independence in 1839 to greater and greater heights.  

Returning to his discussion of Virginia’s lost glory, Hunter returned to the Western canon for inspiration, this time Sir Walter Scott's 1830 novel *The Abbot*. Hunter recited: “the leaves of Virginia's glory, like the holly of Avenel, ‘have been blighted by a half hour's frost,’ he should bear them, in the spirit of the Graeme, ‘so near the sky as to make amends for their stinted growth.’” Avenel here refers to the castle in which the story takes place, and Graeme is the protagonist of the novel.  Graeme serves as page for Lady Avenel, and is initially disliked by servants and others in the castle.  He eventually “makes amends for \[his] stinted growth,” and becomes the Avenel heir.

For Hunter, Virginians should emulate Graeme and become heirs to an American “Avenel” where they will continue to be masters of men. 

Decades later, Hunter resigned his seat as U.S. Senator and became the second Confederate Secretary of State, a position he held from July 25, 1861, to February 18, 1862. He immediately became the Confederate Senator representing Virginia and President Pro Tempore of the Confederate Senate, positions he would hold until the collapse of the Confederate government after Lee's surrender in spring 1865. President Andrew Johnson would pardon him in 1867. From 1874 to 1880, he served as Treasurer of Virginia. Hunter also became active in Confederate memorialization and Lost Cause mythologizing,  publishing *Origin of the Late War. *He would die in Virginia in 1887.
