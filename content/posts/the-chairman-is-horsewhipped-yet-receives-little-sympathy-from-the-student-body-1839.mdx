On March 20th, 1839, having just finished a lecture, Chairman of the Faculty Gessner Harrison was returning to his office when he suddenly became the subject of a shocking and violent degradation.  Two recently expelled students who held a grudge against Harrison for their dismissal—Walter L. Binford and Thomas B. Russell—accosted the Chairman. Binford seized and held Harrison while Russell struck the Chairman several times with a horsewhip. The students had been upbraided in a class by professor Harrison. 

As young men energetically demonstrating their independent mastery and participating as near adults for the first time in the southern culture of honor, Binford and Russell felt unmanned by the professor's in-class humiliation of them. In an attempt to reclaim their manhood and mastery in front of their fellow students, they first challenged professor Harrison to a duel (duels were formalized rites of dispute settlement between equals in the southern culture of honor). 

Harrison, seeing students as his pupils in class and as his charges as Chairman of the Faculty at the University of Virginia, refused the invitation to duel. The refusal to duel for Binford and Russell represented a second humiliation, further angering them and making them more desperate to demonstrate their manliness and mastery so they could bask in the approbation of their peers. Thus, their next action was to assault professor Harrison. They chose the method—horsewhipping—because the whip was the tool for “maintaining due subordination” of inferiors in slave society. They surely thought the only way to reclaim their manhood was to treat Harrison as if he was an insolent enslaved man. 

The Chairman would later express his surprise at having become victim to such a vicious attack, noting in his journal that neither of the men had received what he thought could be construed as any sort of provocation from him that day, or at the time at which they were dismissed from the University. 

At the moment of the assault, a crowd of students soon gathered to watch (again, dueling and horse-whipping worked best in the culture of honor when they had witnesses).  One student eventually decided to intervene, perhaps realizing that allowing the de facto University President to be savagely whipped on the school Grounds was not the best idea. Upon being freed, Harrison immediately rebuked the two dismissed students for their disgraceful actions. Upon hearing these words, Binford and Russell renewed their assault, again horse-whipping Harrison.

Later, having, in the Chairman’s words, “satisfied their purpose of injury,” the two men fled on horseback on the road towards Lynchburg.  UVA swiftly obtained a warrant for their arrest and offered a one hundred dollar award for their capture. Two days later, one pursuer had tracked Binford and Russel down in Nelson County, seizing their horses and baggage. Binford and Russell, however, managed to evade capture at that moment. Suddenly without horses, Binford and Russell decided to return to Charlottesville on foot. Upon their return to the area, they were spotted.  They fled once again, this time with a posse in hot pursuit as they fled in a different direction. That night they were tracked down and confronted in Fluvanna County. Russell was shot by one of the constables attempting to arrest him, but still somehow managed to escape.Binford, though, was captured, escorted back to Charlottesville, and locked up in the local prison.

Upon receiving word of Binford’s apprehension, the Chairman and the Proctor of the University went into town to attend to his trial. They were met at the courthouse by a crowd of around 150 students who had gathered around the jail. The students saw the arrest of Binford as a miscarriage of justice—they understood all too well the culture of mastery that had led to the horse-whipping. The mob there was to support Binford and perhaps even free him. 

As the Chairman later wrote, the students were "threatening an attempt to brake (sic) it open, and all \[were] seemingly ready to commit acts of great extravagance.” Considering both the threatening mob and Binford's expression of regret for the attack on Harrison, University officials decided not to pursue further action against Binford. They agreed that Binford could be released provided he write a letter of apology to Harrison and the university. Students outside of the prison were asked to disperse peacefully. Most did, except for a small group who stopped at local shoemaker Mr. Bailey's store. Bailey had been in the party that had captured Binford in Fluvanna. This smaller group of students attacked Bailey's property as revenge for his participation in bringing Binford to jail.

What happened to Thomas B. Russell is unclear, although the Chairman did, in the days following the incident, grant leave of absence to several students who wished to attend to their wounded friend during his recuperation somewhere out in the country. Russell would never return to UVA.
