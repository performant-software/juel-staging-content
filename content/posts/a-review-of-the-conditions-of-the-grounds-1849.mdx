In June 1849, following the end of the school year, the University ordered an inspection of Grounds to review its cleanliness and good condition. The inspection was requested by the faculty but was overseen by the Proctor. His report was revealing about daily living conditions in the Academical Village at the time. The challenges of keeping the urban settlement clean and healthy were very real in a pre-mechanized world without clean water and sewage systems. Those realities are quite visible in the early history of the early university. 

The official inspection also provides a small glimpse into the work of enslaved people in the day-to-day work of running a university. They were charged with growing and preparing the food, with cooking the meals, with cleaning up after meals for everyone in at least a dozen different kitchens daily, with routine janitorial maintenance of the campus, with cleaning of privies, and disposing of all waste. Reading against the grain, it is easy to see how an overwhelming amount of work fell to enslaved people—they worked far more than sunup to sundown on the campus.

As well, that glimpse into the seemingly unceasing labor of the enslaved everywhere on Grounds, we also hear how white faculty and students viewed that world and how they understood the role of the enslaved in it. Anything they saw or experienced on campus that did not meet their expectations was likely to be blamed on enslaved people. The subtext was usually some combination of white ideas about the enslaved: they were less than competent, they were lazy or unwilling to do their work well, they were themselves the cause of filth and disease. The inspections also did not delve into housing and living conditions for the enslaved people doing most of the work, and never once considered how overwork, abuse, insufficient food, and unhealthy living conditions might contribute to the situation. 

Returning to the report, the disposal of household waste caused considerable problems for what we now think of as sanitation and facilities maintenance. It noted that “where the Proctor requires that soapy water and kitchen ‘slops’ be carted off to remote locations, \[enslaved] servants generally take the easy route by tossing them outside into the gardens and areas surrounding the Pavilions and Hotels. Drains for rainwater runoff are also installed around the buildings, so \[enslaved] servants at times toss the waste down them as well. Unavoidably, the ‘putrescible matter’ soaks down into the soil, where it becomes a ‘fruitful source of offensive exhalations.’” In consequence, the Faculty ruled that all soap suds and kitchen remnants should be sealed in watertight containers that would be taken away six days a week to be dumped on a remote area far from the Lawn and living spaces. The white response to the inspection was to first blame enslaved people for not doing a better job of cleaning or of disposing waste. The second response was to create a system that further burdened the enslaved, as it was the enslaved who would now be moving waste and waste water and disposing of it in some place far from the Academical Village. 

As for the student bathrooms, the faculty noted with some humor that “they are in as good of condition as foul student habits and very poor building construction would allow, and lime is sprinkled into the toilets and on the surrounding ground daily. Because the bathroom floor sits directly on the soil, though, matter is sometimes trapped between the two, causing awful smells.” The faculty hoped to raise the bathroom floors “two feet above the soil level, which would allow for better ventilation.” They additionally noted that the proposal and report this should be shared with the Board of Visitors as soon as possible. The UVA records, in this report and elsewhere, remain mostly silent to how enslaved people were forced to clean out privies (holes in the ground filled with human waste) when they filled or overflowed. 

The report continued: “Pavilion and Hotel gardens and yards are in varying conditions, but the Faculty deems it the responsibility of the Professors and Hotel Keepers to get them in proper shape. The Faculty requests that they be inspected before the week is up for improvements; any that are not already made will be executed by the University \[enslaved] servants and charged for repayment to the residents. In general, lime seems to be the cleaning product of choice and is sprinkled liberally around parts of Grounds that see any type of waste, and whitewash seems a good cover-up on buildings.” Again, it is but a passing reference here, but any “improvements” amounted to significant additional labor for the enslaved people already doing most of the work.
