Maria C. Broadus was UVA professor Gessner Harrison's daughter. She married 1847 UVA alumnus John Albert Broadus, a Baptist minister, on November 14, 1849. In an 1852 letter to her Aunt Mary Jane Harrison, Maria C. Broadus shares updates about family and friends at UVA and in Charlottesville. Her letters provide details about family and social life at the university. 

In that 1852 letter she writes: “Mrs. Stevens and Mrs. McGuffey \[wife of UVA professor William McGuffey] are feeling better, Grandma’s health is improving thanks to the fair weather, Mamma is going to visit her sister” in a carriage that Mr. Rives is driving. She writes her grandmother is “a good deal disturbed that Old Coly \[an enslaved man] has been sold.” The opined that had she had been home, she would have paid to keep Old Coly. Broadus also begs for news of Aunt Caroline’s recent trip, requests that the cellar be filled with produce, and says that her grandmother is just waiting for Mr. Rhodes to take a trip so she can tag along and return home. 

The most intriguing news in this letter might be that a new professor and his wife had just arrived at the University. John Lawrence Smith, an 1837 UVA alumnus, served as a Chemistry professor for only one year (1852-1853). Smith was well-known for his medical studies in Europe and mineral research in Turkey. Everyone expected his wife, the “belle of Louisville,” to be too fine a lady for their humble town, but “on the contrary, she is very simple and easy in her manners and I hope we shall find her a pleasant neighbor.”

Perhaps updates were just so exciting… she had to write them again. In actuality, Maria C. Broadus' letters reveal the contours of epistolary culture at the time. Paper was expensive and any one person regularly wrote to many friends and family on a regular basis. Letters typically followed a basic format, often opening with complaints or concerns about not writing or not receiving letters, then shift into quotidian details about daily life and family events. Thus, Broadus and others often rewrote the same descriptions in other letters written around the same time.

On a separate sheet of paper, Broadus repeated the information she had just shared in the previous letter, at times nearly word-for-word. The dateline and salute are the same. She was writing at least two letters the same day and reported again that Grandma is still waiting on Mr. Rhodes to return her home and “a good deal disturbed” at the sale of enslaved man Old Coly, the family still wants to hear about Aunt Caroline’s trip, the cellar still needs to be filled, and the “apprehension” about the new professor’s wife still reaches the resolution that “she seems to be very ready to make acquaintance and is easy in her manners.” 

Normally, Broadus and others would write different letters around the same time and copy the generic family update material to share it with different recipients. But Broadus made a mistake that day. At the end of the first iteration of the letter is a postscript: “P.S. I must apologize for writing the same thing over twice as I wrote the letter at different times and forgot I had mentioned it before.” She accidentally wrote the same family update to the same recipient, and on the same day. Her letters help us understand the elite culture of letter writing at the time, give us a view of the social life of professors and their families at UVA in the 1850s, and also provide brief but important glimpses into the realities of slavery at UVA.
