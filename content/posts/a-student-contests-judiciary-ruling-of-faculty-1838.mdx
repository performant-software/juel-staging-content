In the winter of 1838, student Henry C. Mayer was expelled for his participation in a major riot on the Lawn. Henry, upset at his quick dismissal, wrote to the faculty to plead for his case for readmission. 

In the letter, Mayer admits to participating in the riot and carefully outlines which violations of school enactments he committed and those which he did not. He attributed his participation to “recklessness and folly,” rather than to any malice against the University itself. Attempting outright flattery, Mayer claimed the University’s unparalleled experience and opportunities were what compelled him to write for readmission, rather than to simply transfer to another school. The plea here strikes a tone rarely heard from students at UVA before 1865. Raised to mastery in a slave society, most students, when confronted with authority, refused to see professors or administrators as their superiors. It was that mindset that led to repeated riots and disturbances at UVA, including the one Mayer had been a participant in. However, when confronted with potential dismissal from the school, even the most haughty students typically struck an uncharacteristically contrite tone. Mayer's letter is one such example. 

Also fascinating in Mayer's letter, however, is the way he questions the faculty’s judiciary processes. Even the written attempt at flattery and contrition still contained a degree of non-apology. Mayer explained that when he was called before the faculty to explain his case, he refused to speak because he thought the faculty would assume a position of “innocence until proven guilty." He claimed he would speak when he had to, but until then he would avoid giving any more evidence than necessary. In reality, this silence was a typical student response to faculty questioning—a refusal to acknowledge the legitimacy of the faculty process and a refusal to say anything that might cause a classmate to be disciplined. 

Unfortunately for Mayer, the faculty, not hearing any compelling reason to find Mayer innnocent or at least guilty of a lesser violation, and likely hearing little from other students whom they interviewed, found him in violation of school enactments and expelled him. For Mayer, though, at least in his letter, it was essentially a “faulty” faculty process that led to his expulsion, not his participation in a riot nor his refusal to defend or explain himself when interviewed. 

Surprisingly, the faculty responded to the letter's overall tone of conciliation and may have ignored Mayer's challenge to the faculty disciplinary process. They voted to readmit Mayer while also upholding the expulsion of another student. By 1838, student riots, disturbances, and protests were part of the annual fabric of life at UVA. In this case, the faculty in readmitting Mayer and upholding another expulsion, reminded students of the necessity for students to follow school laws, and to be especially careful upon their return.
